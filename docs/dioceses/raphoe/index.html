Sunday 15th Novem ber 2026 Thirty-Third Sunday in Ordinary Time Speaking with prisoners in Buenos Aires on Thursday, Pope Leo shared these words, which speak to everyone who has experienced failure and the need for healing and reconciliation: I would like to share with you a reflection on something we all have in common by virtue of being human: the experience of our limitations.

Society often presents everything that constitutes a limitation - incapacity, illness, old age and suffering - as if they were a defect to be corrected, while offering an abundance of possibilities that, in some way, can make us feel “immortal ”. We can see this in certain troubling realities that are all too familiar to us: the sense of omnipotence produced by drugs, the false power that comes from easy money, the cowardly sense of security that comes from inflicting violence upon the weakest, and the impunity that results from corruption.

We often become aware of our limitations through sin and its consequen ces, whether by making a mistake, suffering from an illness or experiencing the death of a loved one.

For this reason, “over the years, we carry within us lessons that leave their mark like scars, the memories of a journey shaped by free dom and failure, dreams and disappointments ”. Nevertheless, “even when persons dehumanize themselves and bring about tragedy, a small light continu es to shine […] one that can be rekindled, with God’s grace, along paths of conversion and reconciliation ”. Dear brothers, this means that even amid the evil, pain and deepest failures you may have experienced or caused, your present circumstances can become a n opportunity to begin your lives anew.

Indeed, human beings do not florish in spite of their limitations, but often through them.

It is precisely when our limitations become tangible in our lives that we can discover a new wisdom, experience the closeness of others, and encounter the presence of the Lord.

For the experience of our limitations, “when truly accepted, does not diminish us but opens us to recognizing the face of God and others ”. Indeed, with God’s grace, it is possible to move beyond total darkness and kindle small lights of hope.

I encourage you to be firm in faith and to anchor your lives in Christian hope , so that you may face the many challenges that arise each day and he lp make this environment more humane and respectful, one that truly fosters rehabilit ation and reintegration.

It is concerned with properties of much greater value, properties that God has entrusted to all of us: life, love, freedom, beauty, truth, and other individual gifts.

These properties do not belong to us. They are entrusted to us by God, and we are accountable for them.

The word ‘entrusted’ is the key to understanding this parable.

God has confidence in us. He trusts us to look after the goods he has placed in our care.

We might say that God has dreams for us far greater than any earthly father or mother have for their children.

While the word talent, as used in today’s gospel, refers to a weight or unit of currency common in the world of Jesus’ time, most people today use it to refer to a person’s particular gifts or abilities of mind or body.

Interpreted in this sense, the parable may help us to reflect on the particular gifts or abilities God has given us, and which he is challenging us to use in the service of his reign.

We should ask ourselves how well we are using these gifts i n the service of our community, parish, and the wider society.

If I were to meet Jesus t oday and he were to ask me: How did you use the talents I gave you?

Who benefited and howdid they benefit from them?

Could I say, with confidence, that I have used them to the best of my ability to promote God’s reign in the world?

If I can honestly say that I have used them to serve others rather than myself, then I have every right to expect to hear the reply of the Master to the productive servants: ‘Well done, good and faithful servant; you have shown you can be faithful in small things; come and join in your master’s happiness’ (Mt 25:21).

Do this in Memory We heartily welcome the 45 children who are preparing for their first Holy Communion this year to this Saturday’s enrollment Mass in St. Crona The Children will reach an age this year where they will be able to understand the transformation that takes place on the Altar through the actions of the priest to change the gifts of bread and wine into the body and the blood of Christ. This is not a sacrament like baptism or confirmation.

We receive those sacraments only once. However, reception of communion and reconciliation this year is the beginning of their ability to receive for the rest of their lives.

Until this point, we do not offer these sacraments to children as this is the age when it is deemed they have developed sufficient reason.

It is a step in the journey through the initiation sacraments.

The faith of parents allows us to baptise young infants into the faith, as the first thing the parents do after naming their child is to promise to bring them up in the faith.

At the age of 8 through their practice of the faith and the catechism that is provided in school, they then can begin to participate in the Eucharist. The last initiation Sacrament is Confirmation.

Simply put, this is the opportunity for the children to make good the promises that were made on their behalf at baptism by their parents and godparents.

Now, they profess their belief in the Creed & receive the fullness of the Holy Spirit.
