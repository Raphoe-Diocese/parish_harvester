Christ the King: Let Christ Reign in Our Hearts On 22 November, we celebrate the Solemnity of Christ the King, marking the end of the liturgical year.

This feast reminds us that Jesus is the King of our lives, whose kingdom is built not on power or wealth but on love, mercy, justice and peace.

He invites us to follow His example by serving others, forgiving one another and showing compassion, especially to those who are poor, lonely and in need.

Message of the Holy Father to young people all over the world for the 41st World Youth Day - 22 November 2026 “Take courage!

I have overcome the world .” (Jn 16:33) Dear Young People , I wish to reach out to each of you with this message and, above all, to give thanks to God for the gift that you are to the Church and to society.

He knows you, loves you and accompanies you on your journey through life.

His Spirit inspires in all of you — different as you are and spread throughout the world — the same desire for things that are great and lasting, and moves you to seek freedom and truth.

From God comes your desire to love and to be loved, as well as the hope that the Holy Spirit awakens in your hearts — a hope that can also become a light for others.

For all of this, I give thanks to the Lord.

We are preparing to celebrate the 41st World Youth Day in our local Churches on 22 November, the Solemnity of Our Lord Jesus Christ, King of the Universe, even as we look forward to our international gathering in Seoul in August 2027. In preparation for these events, I wish to entrust to you the words of Jesus: “Take courage!

I have overcome the world” (Jn 16:33). These words will serve as our theme in the coming months, and I invite you now to begin reǗecting on them with me.

Where does courage come from? Christ’s words begin with an invitation to take courage.

I know well that, at times, instead of courage it is uncertainty or fear that can prevail in us, just as they did in the ǖrst disciples.

Many of you carry questions and concerns that weigh heavily on your hearts.

Perhaps you are anxious about the future, feel pressured to succeed and appear perfect, or experience the stress of constantly comparing yourselves with others or with unattainable, manufactured ideals.

Under the inǗuence of carefully curated images on social media, you may ǖnd it diǘcult to recognize & accept who you truly are.

Indeed, you are longing for genuine affection, since technology cannot replace authentic human closeness but can only imitate empathy.

Some of you know what it means to experience loneliness, disappointment or the fear of not being good enough.

For these reasons, perhaps you too have felt like Jesus’ disciples, crossing the lake at night in a small boat.

The wind grows stronger, the waves rise, and the shore disappears into the darkness.

It is in circumstances like these that we hear the Gospel’s invitation to take courage.

Jesus comes toward his disciples and says to them: “It is I; do not be afraid” (Jn 6:20).

This is the assurance I wish to place in your hearts: even before you begin to seek Christ, he is already seeking you, walking across the troubled waters of life.

Christian courage does not arise primarily from our own strength; it comes from his presence.

Christ himself certainly did not live a life free from storms, nor does he promise us one.

Yet no darkness or suffering is stronger than his love.

He will never abandon you. He invites you into friendship with him and asks you to entrust to him your own journey and that of your communities.

Allow yourselves, then, to be encountered by Jesus Christ who is seeking you, and entrust to him the path you walk, both individually and together.

In this way, you will feel a new and lasting courage growing in your hearts.

The victory over the world The Lord Jesus, who came toward his disciples walking on the water, now says to us: “Take courage!

I have overcome the world” (Jn 16:33). Receive these words as a promise addressed personally to each of you and to the brothers and sisters with whom you share your journey.

More than an exhortation, these words are a gift.

Christ does not say,“You will overcome the world.” He says, “I have overcome the world.” We know that, in this Gospel passage, the “world” does not mean the universe created and loved by God and destined for salvation, but rather the “world of darkness” (cf.

Eph 6:12), which is that mysterious reality, present even in the human heart, that resists and opposes God.

Jesus, & he alone, through his death & Resurrection, has overcome this darkness & reopened for humanity the path to God, to happiness, to justice, & to peace.

We can thus understand other dimensions of the courage to which Jesus calls you.

This courage does not come from your own determination or strength of character.

It comes from trusting in the One who goes before you; it is born of faith in Jesus Christ. When we reǗect on how Christ conquered the world by loving it, we come to understand that Christian courage is not courage against something or someone, but courage forsomething: for life, for truth, for freedom, and for justice.

With the courage that Christ gives us, we can look with new eyes upon the world that God so loved (cf.

Jn 3:16). We, too, have a part to play, however small, in helping transform a “world of darkness” into a “world redeemed.” Christ does not call you to from this world, but to see it through his eyes and touch it with his love, which heals all things.

Allow yourselves to be moved and touched by the reality around you!

Jesus did not love the world from a distance.

He entered fully into our human condition, humbling himself even to the point of death on a cross (cf.

Phil 2:7-8). He experienced joy and friendship, but also hardship, rejection, loneliness and suffering, without ever closing his heart.

You, too, live in a world created good, yet wounded — a world full of possibilities, yet marked by injustice, tensions and fears.

Many long for peace but encounter violence; many yearn for communion but experience loneliness.

Amid these contradictions, Christians are called to follow Christ in his gentle courage: the courage to show love when harshness seems easier and more effective; the courage to seek peace when confrontation appears more advantageous; and the courage to hold fast to hope when cynicism seems more convincing.

The victory of self-giving love When we hear the word “victory,” some might think it means being stronger than others, asserting ourselves or constantly having to prove our worth.

But those are the kinds of victories promoted by the prevailing mentality — the victories of a world without God.

When we look to Jesus, we discover a different kind of victory.

Saint Paul tells us that Christ, though he was in the form of God, emptied himself, taking the form of a servant (cf.

Phil 2:6- 7). The Son of God humbles himself because his love is freely given: he has nothing to prove and nothing to secure for himself.

In humbling himself, he does not lose his dignity; rather, he reveals the greatness and depth of his divine heart, capable of giving life to those who were dead.

His victory is the victory of love that gives itself without counting the cost. Dear young people, true victory does not lie in seeking to prevail over others, but in giving yourselves to them.

By following the path opened by Jesus Christ, you too have no need to impose yourselves, to constantly prove your worth, to appear perfect or to conceal your weaknesses, doubts and limitations.

Have the courage to be your true selves before God and others.

Your dignity does not come from the image you manage to create for yourselves.

You are precious because you have been created in the image of God, known by him and loved by him.

We discover who we truly are when we recognize that our hearts were created for God: to love himand, in him, to love our brothers and sisters.

Indeed, deep within us is the desire to give of ourselves, to devote our lives to the happiness and well-being of others.

This is the freedom Christ offers you: the freedom to move beyond an exclusive concern for yourselves and to open your hearts to the generous creativity of love.

The courage of humility Christ’s self-emptying out of love for us does not end on the cross.

The Father raises the Son from the dead and exalts him above all creation (cf.

Phil 2:9-11). Notice that the Resurrection is not an act of self-exaltation; it is a gift of the Father and his Holy Spirit.

Here we discover another dimension of courage: the courage to receive.

We might call it the courage of humility.

To be friends of Jesus means growing ever more like him in this respect as well.

A certain way of thinking tells us that we must do everything on our own, constantly working to improve ourselves and to develop a stronger personality and character.

Yet this can ultimately lead us to believe that we are our own saviors.

The Gospel shows us another path to growth: not saving ourselves, but allowing ourselves to be saved by Jesus Christ. This requires the courage to accept help from others, acknowledge our limitations, pause, listen and entrust our lives to God.

Life Lessons Life is short. Smile while you still have teeth.
