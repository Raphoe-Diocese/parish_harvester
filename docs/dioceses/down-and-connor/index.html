The first reading tells of the Lord gathering in his flock anc tending to them.

Today we celebrate the last Sunday of the Liturgical Year, known as the Solemnity of Our Lord Jesus Christ, King of the Universe . We began moving toward this goal with the First Sunday of Advent.

Today we have reached it. Given the fact that the Liturgical Year represents our own life in miniature, this experience reminds us, and even more educates us, that we are on a journey toward an encounter with Jesus, the Spouse, when He will come as the King and Lord of life and of history.

The coming we are now awaiting is His second coming.

His first coming was in the humility of a Child laid in a manger.

His second coming will be when He returns in glory at the end of time which we liturgically celebrate today.

But there is also an intermediate coming – we are living it today – in which Jesus is present in us through the Grace we receive in the Sacraments and in the face of every “least one” the Gospel speaks of (“ Unless you turn and become like children, you will not enter the kingdom of heaven” . Mt. 18:3).

We are invited to recognize Jesus in the face of each brother and sister.

Along this journey, the liturgy offers us a school of life to instruct us how to recognize the Lord present in everyday life and to prepare us for His final coming.

For over fifty one Sundays, here in the Church of Holy Cross we have heard the good news of Christ among us declaring over and over again in many ways and by many deeds his unconditional love for us.

We have heard howfar he will go to both embrace and chase us, even when we flee from him.

Today on this final Sunday of the Church’s year the equation is reversed.

We are asked how we have embraced and chased Jesus in the poor and the needy.

It is a sober thought that on the last day we shall be judged.

Someone outside of us will judge us. This very notion grates on our modern sensitivities.

In our value-neutral society where we like to believe that there is no higher standard of judgement than our own self- measurements, this gospel today as the effrontery to remind us that we shall be judged by Another.

We declare this reality of judgement when we pray in the Creed: “He will come to judge the living and the dead” In this Gospel today (Matthew 25:31-46) we are told that we shall be judged in according to our reaction to human need.

God’s judgement does not depend on the knowledge we have gained, or the fame that we have gathered, or on the fortune that we have collected, but on the help we have given.

Jesus tells us it must be help in the simple things - giving a hungry person a meal, a thirsty person a drink, welcoming the stranger, cheering the sick and visiting the prisoner.

These are things that anyone of us can do.

We hear in the Gospel those who helped did not think that they were helping Christ.they helped because they could not stop themselves from helping.

It is the natural, instinctive, quite uncalculating reaction of a loving heart.

Jesus confronts us with this truth, that all such help which is given to Himself, and all such help which is withheld from Him.

If we really wish to delight a parent’s heart, if we really wish to move them to gratitude, then the best way to do it is help his/her child.

God is the great Father, and way to delight the heart of God is to help his children, who are our fellow men and women.

It is a grace that before Mass, we sometimes pray .” 1 have sinned through my own fault, in my thoughts and in my words, in what I have done and in what | HAVE FAILED TO DO” Those good things that we failed to do are called the sins of omission.

In the New Testament, the classic example given by Jesus is the account of the Good Samaritan.

After a man had been beaten and left in need of help, the first two men to pass by—a priest and a Levite, both of whom knew better—failed to act.

The third man, a Samaritan, stopped to show compassion to the man in need (Luke 10:30-37).

There was no sin committed in today’s Gospel against these needy people—they were not intentionally starved or deprived of their clothing.

But the sin of omission was committed when those who could have provided for them chose not to.

Our Lord is the Servant King, the Good Shepherd and shows us the way to act and from Ezekiel in today’s first reading we hear the Lord say: will look after the lost one, bring back the stray, bandage the wounded and make the weak strong.

I shall watch over the fat and healthy. I shall be a true shepherd to them” ( Ezekiel 34:16)

Prayer of St Gertrude the Great Eternal Father, I offer You the most Precious Blood of Your Divine Son, Jesus, in union with all the Masses said throughout the world today, for all the holy souls in purgatory, for sinners everywhere, for sinners in the universal Church, for those in my own home, and within my own family.

Amen. Jesus, Mary, we love you, save souls, save priest’s souls with a plea that we make a thousand times, this act of love, with every breath we take and every heartbeat we make.

Amen The Prayer of St. Gertrude the Great is one of the most beloved Catholic prayers for the souls in purgatory.

St. Gertrude (1256 – 1302), a German Benedictine nun and mystic, received many revelations about God’s mercy and the afterlife.

According to tradition, Our Lord told her that this prayer would release many souls from purgatory each time it is recited.
