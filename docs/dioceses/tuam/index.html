Thirty –Second Sunday in Ordinary Time In today’s Gospel, Jesus draws from this revelation about the future the practical moral that a Christian needs to be on the watch, living each day as if it were his last. The important thing is not to speculate about when these events will happen and what form they will take, but to live in such a way that they find us in a state of grace.

Living each day as if it were your last is a powerful concept that c an help us to live more fully and with greater purpose.

It reminds us that our time on this earth is limited and that we should make the most of every moment.

It also encourages us to focus on what is truly important in such as our relationships with others and our relationship with God.

Keeping watch is another important aspect of this passage.

It reminds us to vigilant and to stay alert to the signs of the times.

We should be aware of the world arou nd us and the events that are unfolding, so that we can respond appropriately.

This means being informed about what is happening in our communities, our nation, and the world, and taking action when necessary.

Today’s Gospel , then, is a powerful reminder to Christians to live each day as if it were their last and to keep watch for the signs of the times.

By doing so, we can live more fully and with greater purpose, and we can be prepared for whatever the future may hold for us.

Louisburgh Church Restoration and Repair works Since 2015 the south wall of the church and the bel l tower have suffered from significant water penetration (a problem first noted in the 1950s).

Internal render is breaking down, and paintwork is peeling off the walls.

Humidity levels in the building are high and water saturation of walls is reducing thermal performance making the building difficult to heat and maintain.

Investigative repo rts were carried out to identify and remedy the sources of moisture penetration.

These included a measurement survey and Structural , Stained Glass, Services, Asbestos, and Building Condition reports.

Temporary screens were erected to test the performance of the wall structure.

A specialist conservation contractor carried out drill testing o f the walls which confirmed significant water-logging of wall cores.

A Conservation Management Plan was commissioned to guide the remedial works and to aid in making applications for grant funding.

In February 2026 an application was made to Mayo County Council for permission to carry out repair works to remedy wall damp initially on 2 bays of the west wall, replace the bell tower roof and resurface the gravel path connecting the two entrances.

Fraternal Support The life of a priest is never dull.

While the of parish life is constant – with regular Masses to be celebrated, sermons to be written, homes to be visited, newsletters to be prepared, schools to be dropped into and meetings to be attended, (not to mention diocesan commitments) – it is often the unexpected which adds colour and diversion.

While baptisms and weddings are organised weeks/months in advance funerals can never be planned for – as our former Archbishop Joe Cassidy put it ‘death whether it is expected or not is nearly always unaccommodating’. Then there are the practical unforeseens and unexpected such as a leak in one of the churches, the heating not working, bulbs needing to be replaced, etc.

Nowadays administrivia tasks take up a lot of a priest’s time and energy.

There are annual accounts to be prepared and stringent audits to be undergone in the areas Finance, Safeguarding and While initiatives like the CHY3 tax reclaim scheme are hugely beneficial to the parish they bring with them strict conditions.

Each year Risk Evaluations are required for insurance and health and safety.

Occasionally major undertakings, like the recent Tower Project in the Parish church can be stressful and consume huge amounts of time and cause no small amount of pressure.

While groups such as the Parish Pastoral Council and the Parish Finance Committee are hugely helpful and supportive in all of the above in the end much of the responsibility for the running of a parish falls back on the local priest. For the past twenty five years I have been part of a group of priests who meet every 5/6 weeks to chat and offer support to each other.

It is a peer supervision group which provides an opportunity for each of us to discuss and chat through any area of concern which may be troubling us.

We were originally a group of 8 but sadly over the years three of our group have died.

In order to get the best use of our time together we engage a trained counsellor who facilitates the meeting.

Our meetings always follow the same format; we begin with some prayer time, then we check in with each other; how are we feeling?

How has life been since we last met. If anyone has a particular issue/experience they would like to have discussed it is mentioned and after each priest has checked in we take each of the issues raised in turn.

Issues can vary from seeking advice on how to deal with a particular pastoral situation to help with regard to some matter relating to church property.

We listen caringly, offer advice and support sensitively and try our best to help each other to be better priests.

The life of a priest is never dull and as his area of pastoral care grows ever wider and the responsibility involved becomes ever heavier he can easily feel alone and isolated.

I thank God for the support and encouragement over 25 years of my fellow priests.
