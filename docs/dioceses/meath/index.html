Do you keep saying “later” to God, while the busy things take up every hour? The wedding banquet used in todays Gospel points to the joy at the end of all things, where God and His people are one and grief is over. It is the best news there has ever b een. And now watch what the invited guests do with it. They will not come. The king sends servants to call them, and they make ligh t of it. One goes off to his farm, another to his bu siness. They are busy with things to do, and the feast can wait, and so they dec line the one invitation that outlasts the grave. Th is the quiet tragedy, and it is ours more than we like to admit. God is rarely r efused by dramatic rejection. He is refused by dist raction. By good and ordinary things, the work, the errands, the screen, the thousand tasks that are not sins but that silently fill every hour the feast was meant to have. We do not say no to God. We say later. We say when things settle. So the king does something astonishing. He says, the feast is ready, but those invited were not worthy. G o out to the roads, to the edges of town, and invite everyone you find, both bad and good, until the hall is full. The ones toobusy miss it. The ones with no claim at all, the poor , the overlooked, the sinners from the street corners, are carried in to the wedding. This is the mercy underneath the whole parable. When the comfortable decline, God does not cancel the feast. He widens The people who know they are hungry come ru nning, while the people who thought they had better things to do stay home with their better things. The feast is free, but it is not cheap. We are all invited exactly as we are, off the street, unwort hy, carrying our sins. However, we cannot come to the wedding of the Lamb and insist on staying exactly as we walked in. To a ccept the invitation is to accept being changed by it. The deepest hunger in us, the one no farm or business or busy hour can touch, is the hunger for this feast, for God Himself. So invitation is in your hands today, as real as it was on Isaiah’s mountain. The feast that swallows up death is prepared. The only question is whether we will come, or send our regrets and go back to being busy. The table is set. The hall is filling. Return fare €10. To book a seat, phone or text PJ 087 6386270 or Regina 086 8510238 “If anyone thirst, let him come to me and drink ” (Jn 7:37). Brothers and sisters, in our thirst, we turn toward Christ, the source of living water: water that quenches our thirst and makes life flourish, the water of God’s love “poured into our hearts through the Holy Spirit who has been given to us ” (Rom 5:5). The desire for God dwells deep within our hearts, and we cry out with the psalmist: “My soul thirsts for God, for the living God ” (Ps 2:2). This is the cry of a humble and passionate heart whose deepest, most existential thirst continues to grow. And it is Jesus Christ, through his Incarnation, who comes to meet every person in the midst of his or her own story, and reveals himself as the true source of living water capable of quenching our thirst. This thirst that dw ells in every human heart is not so much a thirst for material things, but a thirst for meaning, for truth a nd fulfilment. Jesus responds to our profound desire for relationship, for communion with God, in whom the full ness of life forever resides. ………. Yes, brothers and sisters, Jesus Christ, the Good Shepherd, leads us, as a shep herd leads his sheep, to the source of happiness and peace. He knows each one of us by name. Nothing about us is hidden from him – neither our secrets, nor our intentions nor our weaknesses. He searches the depths of hearts. He gave his life for us and continues to give it to us through the sacraments. carries the weary and wounded sheep on his shoulders, and gently brings back those who have gone astray. Jesus reveals God’s unconditional love for us. In his eyes, none of us is merely a number or an entry in a register, but a unique and irreplaceable person. From the Homily of Pope Leo, while in France 26th Sept. 2026 Of all the forms of human love, that between a man and a woman holds a special place. It can strike the heart like an arrow – intensely and swiftly. Yet true love does not stop there. It is not merely a fleeting, briefly inflamed emotion. It is more. It is a deep commitment to the other, a decision, a journey and a covenant for life. “Love is authentic and can last forever only when it reflects the eternal splendour of God – God who is love (cf. 1 Jn 4:8). Solid and fruitful relationships are built together on mutual trust, on this ‘forever’ that is the beating heart of every vocation to family life and religious consecration ” (Address during the meeting with young people in Lebanon). This love asks first: “How can I love as Christ loved? ” (cf. Jn 15:12) and puts the question “What do I get out of it? ” last. Pope Leo, blessing newly married couples. On this Prisoners’ Sunday, we are reminded that no one should be forgotten, abandoned or defined only by their past. On 26 December 1958, Pope St John XXIII visited the Regina Coeli prison in Rome. In a gesture of solidarity, he said to the prisoners: “You could not come to me, so I came to you ”. May the words of St John XXIII on this his memorial (11th October) — “I came to you ” — inspire the People of God to draw near to those who are in prison. Pope Leo XIV, speaking at the Jubilee of Prisoners last year, reminded us that “no human being is defined only by his or her actions ” and that “justice is always a process of reparation and reconciliation. ” These words challenge us to see every prisoner first as a person — a per-son with dignity, wounds, hopes and the possibility of a new beginning. Pope Leo also urged us:“we must never tire, be discouraged or give up ”. Even when prison is a difficult place and obstacles seem overwhelming, we are called to continue “with tenacity, courage and a spirit of collaboration”. There can be beauty even in difficult places. As Pope Leo expressed it, when we preserve “mercy and forgiveness, beautiful flowers spring forth from the ‘hard ground’ of sin and suffering ”. On this Prisoners’ Sunday, we remember prisoners and their families especially children and young people im- pacted by imprisonment. We pray for prison officers and staff, chaplains, volunteers and all who work for rehabilitation and restoration. We remember, too, those carrying the wounds of the past and those struggling to believe that change is possible. Youth Group - COMING SOON!
