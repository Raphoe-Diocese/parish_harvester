A LITANY OF REMEMBRANCE – WE REMEMBER THEM In the rising of the sun and in its going down we remember them; In the blowing of the wind and in the chill of winter we remember them; In the opening of the buds and in the warmth of summer we remember them; In the rustling of the leaves and the beauty of the autumn we remember them; In the beginning of the year and when it ends, we remember them; When we are weary and in need of strength, we remember them; When we have joys we yearn to share, we remember them.

So long as we live, they too shall live for they are now a part of us as we remember them.

In our Gospel this week when Jesus was asked which was the greatest commandment of the law of Moses, he answered, not with his own words or ideas, but with the words of God to Moses and with the words he himself, as a Jewi shman, prayed twice each day.

We hear them in the Gospel as God’s word to us as Christians.

If we are being called in our present world to do one thing – then it has to be the call to proclaim love and hope in a world increasingly hearing calls to and fear.

All around us, in midterm elections, in riots in European cities, in wars ravaging whole regions of our world, in tariff wars, in soaring gas and diesel prices, in rising inflation and cost of living, leaders and politicians and those with vested int erests in unrest and polarisation, sow seeds of hate and use fear as a we apon to divide people.

Never before has this Gospel been more pertinent and relevant to our times.

We live within a divided and fearful world, but we should not give our alleg iance to a divided and fearful world.

From the National Synodal Assembly In answer to the question, “What does God want of the Church at this time?”, t he proposals suggest at first glance what God m ight be asking us to do.

But there is something else! They are also pointing to what God might be asking us to be.

Or what God might be asking us to become.

Actions can too easily be put in a corner and be seen as the responsibility of someone else.

But becoming is something that belongs to us all.

It’s about our shared identity – not just about what we do, but who we are.

It’s about growth. It’s about conversion.

It’s about becoming the People that God wants us to be.

Actions can be decided, implemented, and completed.

But growth, change, conversion, becoming – are a journey over time.

A journey of becoming a Church that proclaims the Gospel a Church that is a community of belonging a Church that is a place of healing a Church that accompanies a Church that empowers for ministry and co-responsibility.

The proposals now find their place in a vision of the Church that we are seeking to become.

And we can all find ourselves in this vision of the Church.

The pathways offer a real alternative to the polarised and polarising discourse that characterises so much of the life of our Church and our w orld.

They offer us a way of being together. And long after recommendations from today have been decided, implemented, evaluated, the p athways will continue to offer us ways of becoming, ways of finding our identity, and ways of walking the path together.
