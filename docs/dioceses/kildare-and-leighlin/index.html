KILDARE AND LEIGHLIN DIOCESEAND AND Day of Prayer for Survivors and Victims of Abuse Friday February 20th 2026 (1st Friday of Lent 2026) - Our annual Holy Hour for Survivors of Abuse was held in the Cathedral Parish in Carlow.

Thanks to all who attended and participated in the service.

Candle of Atonement Prayer Lord, forgive us our many sins.

We grieve and repent with all our hearts for having offended you, for our great failings and neglect of the young and vulnerable.

We place all of those who have been hurt by the Church in any way into your loving hands and under the protection of Our Blessed Mother.

Lord, bring peace to their broken lives and show us all the way out of darkness and into the light of your Word.

May we as the people of God be more fully human, more fully Christ-like and more fully your people, that we may see the errors of the past and go forward with renewed hope and faith in Christ and in our Church.

Amen. Testimonials from Young People who engage in youth activities in our Diocese.

Message from Bishop Denis Nulty Sunday, February 22, is Diocesan Safeguarding Sunday.

It is the first Sunday of Lent and appropriately so, as we enter the Penitential Season and seek forgiveness for past failings.

In his address at the January Consistory Pope Leo XIV reminded all that “abuse itself causes a deep wound, which may last a lifetime; but often the scandal in the Church is that the door was closed and victims were not welcomed or accompanied with the closeness of authentic pastors”. This year we have intentionally linked the ‘Day of Prayer for Survivors and Victims of Abuse’ on Friday, February 20 with ‘Safeguarding Sunday’ two days later.

The National Child Safeguarding Policy for our Church is called ‘A Safe and Welcoming Church’ and that is what our diocese is and our parishes are, a safe and welcoming Church.

The fact that ‘Safeguarding Sunday’ has become such a regular part of our Diocesan and Parish life, demonstrates how the safeguarding of children and vulnerable adults has been so well embedded into every area of the life of our diocese, its 56 parishes and 117 faith communities.

Safeguarding Sunday is once again an opportunity to acknowledge and recognise the years of dedication and work by so many to ensure that Kildare & Leighlin Diocese is a safe place for children and all who work with them.

It’s an opportunity to thank all who today ensure safeguarding is so well embedded in our diocesan culture, Ailish Higgins, our Director of Safeguarding, currently on maternity leave, Michele Hughes, our Acting Designated Liaison Person, the Diocesan Safeguarding Committee, our Parish Safeguarding Representatives, our priests, our deacons and our people, particularly the many who volunteer at parish level, ensuring the life and vitality of their faith community.

As ever, on my parish visits, I encourage ministry with children and young people, this is an essential part of the mission of the Church.

Where altar serving, youth choirs, Sunday School have returned I welcome them warmly; where they haven’t for whatever reason, once again I encourage their early return.

The life of a parish is often defined by the active engagement of young people in that parish.

Safeguarding Sunday is a reassurance to all that being involved in the parish is governed by best practices in safeguarding.

Every blessing & thanks, Bishop of Kildare and Leighlin +Denis Nulty If you have a safeguarding concern, please contact: Diocesan Designated Liaison Person (DLP): Ailish Higgins

Then I heard how many were sealed: a hundred and forty-four thousand, out of all the tribes of Israel.

After that I saw a huge number, impossible to count, of people from every nation, race, tribe and language; they were standing in front of the throne and in front of the Lamb, dressed in white robes and holding palms in their hands.

They shouted aloud, ‘Victory to our God, who sits on the throne, and to the Lamb!

And all the angels who were standing in a circle round the throne, surrounding the elders and the four animals, prostrated themselves before the throne, and touched the ground with their foreheads, worshipping God with these words, ‘Amen.

As we do so we reawaken precious memories—happy times spent together.

For some of us, and particularly those who are recently bereaved, such an activity can be painful and evoke feelings of great sadness and loss.

In such moments the Lord is close to us.

Mary is donating two Icons , ‘The Annunciation’ and ‘Mother and Child’ to Killeen Church and has considered it a most profound honour that the Parish is accepting these for permanent display in Killeen.

Mary has lived in Switzerland for over 50 years and has trained in Iconography under a Russian expert in Lausanne.

Mary created theses Icons using the same type materials, paints and gold leaf as was used during the creation of the original Icons.

Message from Bishop Denis Nulty Sunday, February 22, is Diocesan Safeguarding Sunday.

It is the first Sunday of Lent and appropriately so, as we enter the Penitential Season and seek forgiveness for past failings.

In his address at the January Consistory Pope Leo XIV reminded all that “abuse itself causes a deep wound, which may last a lifetime; but often the scandal in the Church is that the door was closed and victims were not welcomed or accompanied with the closeness of authentic pastors”. This year we have intentionally linked the ‘Day of Prayer for Survivors and Victims of Abuse’ on Friday, February 20 with ‘Safeguarding Sunday’ two days later.

The National Child Safeguarding Policy for our Church is called ‘A Safe and Welcoming Church’ and that is what our diocese is and our parishes are, a safe and welcoming Church.

The fact that ‘Safeguarding Sunday’ has become such a regular part of our Diocesan and Parish life, demonstrates how the safeguarding of children and vulnerable adults has been so well embedded into every area of the life of our diocese, its 56 parishes and 117 faith communities.

Safeguarding Sunday is once again an opportunity to acknowledge and recognise the years of dedication and work by so many to ensure that Kildare & Leighlin Diocese is a safe place for children and all who work with them.

It’s an opportunity to thank all who today ensure safeguarding is so well embedded in our diocesan culture, Ailish Higgins, our Director of Safeguarding, currently on maternity leave, Michele Hughes, our Acting Designated Liaison Person, the Diocesan Safeguarding Committee, our Parish Safeguarding Representatives, our priests, our deacons and our people, particularly the many who volunteer at parish level, ensuring the life and vitality of their faith community.

As ever, on my parish visits, I encourage ministry with children and young people, this is an essential part of the mission of the Church.

Where altar serving, youth choirs, Sunday School have returned I welcome them warmly; where they haven’t for whatever reason, once again I encourage their early return.

The life of a parish is often defined by the active engagement of young people in that parish.

Safeguarding Sunday is a reassurance to all that being involved in the parish is governed by best practices in safeguarding.

Not fame or extraordinary achievements, but a life shaped by God’s love.

On the Solemnity of All Saints, we celebrate holy women and men whose lives reveal that love —and remember that we share their calling.

St John reminds us that we are God’s children.

This is where holiness begins: not in proving ourselves worthy, but in receiving the Father’s love and allowing it to change how we live.

The saints let Christ’s love shape their choices, relationships and prayer.

We are invited to do the same. Holiness takes root in ordinary moments: speaking kindly to someone who has hurt us, making time for a lonely neighbour, welcoming someone others overlook, or standing beside a person being bullied.

The Beatitudes show us the way. Jesus calls us to be gentle, merciful and peacemakers; to hunger for what is right; and to put others before our pride.

These are not ideals reserved for a few exceptional people.

They are a daily invitation to every Christian.

Choosing love can be costly, especially when blame or indifference seems easier.

Yet we do not walk this path alone. The Holy Spirit helps us live in Christ and reminds us that God is our Father.

All Saints points us towards heaven, where we shall see God face to face.

Each loving choice offers a glimpse of that joynow.

The saints’ invitation is ours too: let God’s love become visible through you.

Message from Bishop Denis Nulty Sunday, February 22, is Diocesan Safeguarding Sunday.

It is the first Sunday of Lent and appropriately so, as we enter the Penitential Season and seek forgiveness for past failings.

In his address at the January Consistory Pope Leo XIV reminded all that “abuse itself causes a deep wound, which may last a lifetime; but often the scandal in the Church is that the door was closed and victims were not welcomed or accompanied with the closeness of authentic pastors”. This year we have intentionally linked the ‘Day of Prayer for Survivors and Victims of Abuse’ on Friday, February 20 with ‘Safeguarding Sunday’ two days later.

The National Child Safeguarding Policy for our Church is called ‘A Safe and Welcoming Church’ and that is what our diocese is and our parishes are, a safe and welcoming Church.

The fact that ‘Safeguarding Sunday’ has become such a regular part of our Diocesan and Parish life, demonstrates how the safeguarding of children and vulnerable adults has been so well embedded into every area of the life of our diocese, its 56 parishes and 117 faith communities.

Safeguarding Sunday is once again an opportunity to acknowledge and recognise the years of dedication and work by so many to ensure that Kildare & Leighlin Diocese is a safe place for children and all who work with them.

It’s an opportunity to thank all who today ensure safeguarding is so well embedded in our diocesan culture, Ailish Higgins, our Director of Safeguarding, currently on maternity leave, Michele Hughes, our Acting Designated Liaison Person, the Diocesan Safeguarding Committee, our Parish Safeguarding Representatives, our priests, our deacons and our people, particularly the many who volunteer at parish level, ensuring the life and vitality of their faith community.

The life of a parish is often defined by the active engagement of young people in that parish.

Safeguarding Sunday is a reassurance to all that being involved in the parish is governed by best practices in safeguarding.

It is the first Sunday of Lent and appropriately so, as we enter the Penitential Season and seek forgiveness for past failings.

In his address at the January Consistory Pope Leo XIV reminded all that “abuse itself causes a deep wound, which may last a lifetime; but often the scandal in the Church is that the door was closed and victims were not welcomed or accompanied with the closeness of authentic pastors”. Chicago math teacher. Devout Catholic. Accepted into Harvard Law.

He had everything a young man could dream of, But then—he made a decision that no one saw coming.

He said no to Harvard No to a six-figure future.

No to fame. No to comfort And yes—to something few dare to choose: A life of complete surrender.

He j oined a missionary group and moved to Peru.

Not to the cities. Not to the tour ist spots . But to the *most* remote villages—where children die from treatable diseases.

And families walk miles just for clean water.

There were no roads. No running water. No Just mountains.

Silence And poverty. But he embraced it like home Robert didn’t just live *among* the people.

He became *one* of them Learned Quechua—the sacred language of the Incas Carried food on foot for days Slept on dirt floors with the villagers Prayed under the stars When he wasn’t building shelters.

He taught math to barefoot kids under broken rooftops.

When he wasn’t teaching. He carried the sick on donkeys to get help.

When he wasn’t healing. He listened—truly listened—to stories no one else cared to h ear.

While his friends from back home became lawyers and doctors.

He became something else entirely. A shepherd A brother A quiet warrior of faith And slowly—his legend grew.

His acts weren’t broadcasted. But they echoed throu gh the Andes Bishops noticed Priests noticed And eventually—the Vatican noticed They called him back to lead his entire Augustinian order.

From serving a vil lage. To overseeing 2,800 brothers in over 40 countries.

Still—he kept his same sandals Still—he walked with the poor Still—he rejected luxury Then came the call that changed everything: Rome wanted him closer.

In 2020 He was appointed Archbishop and assigned to govern other bishops globally.

It was rare. But Robert had never chased traditi on.

He wasn’t just fluent in Latin or Canon Law.

He was fluent in *compassion* In humility In listening In *presence* The Vatican didn’t just see a priest. They saw a *leader with soul*. On September 30, 2023. Pope Francis made it official: Robert Prevost was named a Cardinal.

Just one step below the Pope And then… in 2025 History was made.

For the first time ever An American A former math teacher A missionary to the forgotten Was elevated as the 267th Pope of the Catholic Church And he didn’t forget the people who shaped him.

To this day [the day he became Pope] Robert still returns to the same villages.

Still prays in Quechua Still sits on dirt floors Still holds the hands of the elderly in silence Because leadership he believes:

But Robert Prevost proves: Titles mean nothing without service Knowledge is useless without love And faith—without sacrifice—is noise He turned down the world.

Parish Newsletter 1st November 2026 Feast of All Saints No: 2068 Mt 5:1-12 November …. A Month to Remember …. November is a special time in our parish calendar as we pause to remember and pray for our loved ones who have gone before us.

While we feel the sadness of their absence, we do not grieve without hope.

As people of faith, we are sustained by a powerful promise of the Resurrection.

The belief that Jesus shares in our human nature, for he lived as we live, he died as we will die.

And, in his dying the hope and promise of Resurrection.

Cornerstone of our Christian faith. We carry in our hearts a promise, a belief that life is changed, not ended, and that one day we will be reunited in love.

Throughout this month, all our loved ones will be remembered in our prayers and our Masses.

These are the names written upon the ‘November List of the Dead’ and placed in reverence before the Altar of God.

In November we pray for the Holy Souls in Purgatory.

We visit the graves, the resting place of our loved ones, and we light candles in remembrance.

These are all beautiful traditions of our faith and they offer fitting testament to the memory of our loved ones.

May they Rest in Peace. May the Lord support us all the day long, Till the shades lengthen and the evening comes, and the busy world is hushed, and the fever of life is over, and our work is done.

But is this true? Do we really have to waypay for everything we do?

Mary Jo Leddy, in her wonderful book on gratitude, claims that one of the great principles innate within reality itself is this: “/T_heair you breathe into the universe is the air that it will breathe back and if your energy is right, it will renew itself even as you give it away.” In essence, that’s the law of karma, a mystery expressed in different ways in all the great religions of the world.

If that’s true, and it is, it explains a lot of things (though not necessarily to our liking).

Why are we inhaling so much bitter air? Perhaps it has to do with the air we’re breathing out.

Feast of All Saints Deacon: Rev. Liam Dunne Matthew 5:1-12 What makes a saint In 1987, Dr Paul Farmer founded Partners in Health in Haiti.

Its mission was to provide high-quality healthcare to patients from impoverished backgrounds and those who lived far from healthcare facilities.

Over the following thirty years, the organization exp anded across Africa, Latin America, Russia and the Navajo Nation in the US. Dr Farmer’s goal was to make the latest sophisticated treatments, which were available only in wealthy regions, available to all people.

He was instrumental in bringing lifesaving HIV drugs to the people of Haiti in the early 2000s and was later involved in founding a medical school in Rwanda, where he had been teaching before he passed away unexpectedly in February 2022. Described as a ‘global health champion’, Dr Farmer’s driving forces were equality, solidarity and love.

His central philosophy could be summed up in his own words: ‘The idea that some lives matter less is the root of all that is wrong with the world.

Dr Farmer was a social justice campaigner, what some might call a modern saint.

Aware of the disparities around the world, he set about finding creative ways to narrow the gap.

Saints are all around us, teaching us about God through their actions.

They persevere in all they do for their communities and those on the margins.

They are the blessed ones. The Paul Farmers of this world inspire us to strive to live the Beati tudes of Jesus in our daily lives.

The Beatitudes are an urgent call to honour the dignity of all people, especially those whose suffer.

The most pressing need of our time is to bridge the gap between the haves and the haves-nots.

When we hear of the disparity between the income and lifestyles of the super-rich and the poorest people in the world, it can be hard to stomach.

Becoming aware of our privilege is the first step towards solidarity.

The Beatitudes call us to put our hunger for justice into action.

Perhaps that is the making of all saints.
