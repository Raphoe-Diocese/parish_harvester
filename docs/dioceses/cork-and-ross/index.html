Over 135 years ago, Leo XIII published Rerum Novarum on workers’ rights.

But back through the centuries was another Leo, always called ‘the Great’. This quiet scholarly man was the one who met Attila the Hun at the gates of Rome and persuaded the attacker to go away.

Leo the Great courageously worked for peace, as our Leo also does.

Is there a hell? A few years ago, the Church revised the words of the Creed.

That prayer used to speak of Christ ‘descending to the dead’ after His own death.

In the revised wording, Christ ‘descended into HELL’. Some found this unsettling, till they remembered how much hell persists on earth; where there is war and destruction, rape and all the terrible abuse of people.

Christ descended to those dark places and remains, defeating darkness with his redeeming power.

When life here is over, is there a hell in the afterlife too?

If there is light, there must be shade, if there is heaven, there must be hell too.

But how many people does the merciful God permit to enter it?

Only God knows. The catechism puts it succinctly: “God predestines no one to go to hell; for this, a wilful turning away from God is necessary, and persistence in it to the end.” 1037) And how likely is that, when a person leaving this world meets the love of God, perhaps for the first time?

Can the depths of evil be swept away in that moment, through the victory Christ has won?

This mystery will be revealed only in eternity.

Michael Kelly, Aid to the Church in Need Ireland, w rites in Intercom Magazine… PERSEVERING IN FAITH AMID PERSECUTION This Sunday’s readings, as we mark the beginning of t he Week of Witness dedicated to praying for Christians persecuted for their faith, speak of trials and endurance.

As we reflect on the readings , we focus on Christians persecuted for their faith, trusting in God’s promise of ultimate victory.

St. Paul, in his letter to the Thessalonians, speaks directly to people who may have to pay a price for staying faithful.

He sa ys that the day of the Lord will come like a thief.

The world around them t alks of peace and security.

Believers are not to share that drowsiness.

They are children of light, meant to stay awake and sober.

In any agewhen faith becomes costly, that sobriety is not gloom.

It is simply the refusal to pretend that everything is fine, or that discipleship can be kept safely out of sight.

The parable of the talents presses the same point From another side.

The servant who buries what he has been given does so o ut of fear.

He knows the master is demanding, so he decides the safest thing is to do nothing and hand the silver back untouched.

Fear of loss, f ear of the master’s severity, fear of standing out – any of these can send a gift underground.

For Christians who live where faith is watched, rest ricted, or punished, the temptation is real: keep the light low, say little, r isk nothing, preserve what you can.

Neither reading promises that faithfulness will be easy or rewarded at once.

What they do promise is that staying awake and putting the gift to work belong to the life of faith itself.

The persecuted Church is not asked to be reckless.

It is asked not to bury what it has been given simply because the night feels long and the risk feels high.

The charity Aid to the Church in Need tells us that some 413 million Christians live in countries where religious freedo m is severely violated – that’s 18% or about one in five Christians.

Of these , approximately 220 million live in countries where they are directlye xposed to persecution – that’s 10% of all Christians.

Let’s keep them in our prayers this weekend.

Without love, there would be no grief, nor the blessings promised in today’s Gospel.

So let us find comfort in knowing that our loved ones are at rest with the Lord, and in the support, prayers and care of those around us.

Let us give thanks for the love we have shared, and treasure the memories we carry in our hearts.
