Letter from Bishop Kevin Doran To: The people of Keash Parish. ‘Dear Parishioners of Keash Parish, I am writing to let you know that I have asked Fr. Gabriel Murphy to take on an additional assignment, while remaining Parish Priest of Keash.

He has kindly agreed. The background to my request is that, five years ago, I asked Deacon Damien Kearns to accept an appointment a s Administrator of the Parish of Aughanagh (Elphin Diocese), with the support of Fr. Larry Behan as Sacramental Minister.

Both men exercised their ministry on a purely voluntary basis, accepting only expenses personally incurred in the exercise of their ministry.

I take this opportunity to thank them on your behalf for their very generous commitment.

Some Deacon Damien informed me that he needed to step back from his role in the parish and to focus his energy on his role as Chaplain at ATUSt. Angela’s, which he provides on a voluntary basis, alongside his professional employment at the University.

Fr. Larry Behan has indicated his availability to continue as Sacramental Minister in Aughanagh, and I am very grateful to him for that.

The Canon Law of the Church requires that the administration of the parish is overseen by a Priest. For reasons as of health, Fr. Larry is not in a pos ition to take on that responsibility, and I fully respect that.

For some months now, I have been exploring various possibilities for the future of Aughanagh parish, including the possibility of joining the parish with a neighbouring parish.

Having considered various options, I came to the conclusion that the best partnership would be between Aughanagh and Keash.

What this means, in practice, is that Fr Gabriel will be Parish Priest of both Parishes and will take responsibility for administration.

Fr. Gabriel and Fr. Larry will work together as a team to celebrate Mass and the Sacraments across the tw o parishes.

The new arrangement will become effective from the weekend of

Saturday 14th and

Sunday 15th November. Our hope is that, in the coming weeks, we will be able to appoint a shared Parish Secretary on a part-time basis.

There is something rather unique in this arrangement, because it involves a new kind of relationship betwe en two parishes, one of which is in Achonry and one of which is in Elphin.

This reflects the new relationship betwe en the two Dioceses, which are joined “in persona episcopi” and is made possible by the work that has been done over the last two years, in bringing the two Dioceses into a closer practical and pastoral relations hip.

The two parishes of Aughanagh and Keash will remain distinct, each having its own Parish Pastoral Council and its own Parish Finance Council.

It is my hope, however, that the parishioners, working in partnership with thei r priests, will recognise and embrace the opportunity to draw closer and to share pastoral initiatives, both within each parish and between the two parishes.

This will not just avoid unnecessary duplication but wil l also allow for a richer sharing of spiritual and pastoral gifts.

Yours Sincerely, Kevin Doran Bishop of Achonry and of Elphin Knockbrack Cemetery – Update.

All Souls

Commemoration of the faithful departed.

Belief in the resurrection of the dead is an essential part of Christian revelation. It implies a particular understanding of the ineluctable mystery of death.

Death is the end of earthly life, but “not of our existence” (St Ambrose) since the soul is immortal. “Our lives are measured by time, in the course of which we change, grow old, and, as with all living beings on earth, death seems like the normal end of life.(CCC 1007) Seen from the perspective of the faith, “death is the end of man’s earthly pilgrimage, of the time of grace and mercy which God offers him so as to work out his earthly life in keeping with the divine plan, and to decide his ultimate destiny (CCC 1013)

In one light, death can seem natural, in another it can be seen as “the wages of sin”(Rm 6.23).

Authentically interpreting the meaning of Scripture (cf Jn 2:17 , 3;3 , 3:19; Wis 1:13, Rm 5:12, 6:23), the Church teaches that “death entered the world on account of man’s sin”(CCC 1008) Jesus, the Son of God. “born of a woman and subject to the Law”(Gal 4:4) underwent death which is part of the human condition; despite his anguish in the face of death (Mk 14:33-34, Heb 5:7-8), “he accepted it in an act of complete and free submission to his Father’s will.

The obedience of Jesus has transformed the curse of death into a blessing” (CCC 1009)

Death is the passage to the fullness of true life.

The church, subverting the logic of this world, calls the christian’s day of deaths his dies natalis, the day of his heavenly birth, where ‘there will be no more death, and no more mourning or sadness the world of the past has gone’ (Apos 21:4).

Death is the prolongation , in a new way of life as the liturgy says ‘For your faithful, O Lord life has changed not ended; while our earthly dwelling is destroyed, a new and eternal dwelling is prepared for us in Heaven’ (Missal Preface).

The death of a Christian is an event of grace, having, as it does, a positive value and significance in Christ and through Christ. Scripture teaches that ‘Life to me, of course, life is Christ, but death would bring me something more’ (Phil 1:21); here is a saying you can rely on:‘if we have died with him, then we shall live with him’ (2Tm 2:11).

According to the faith of the church, ‘todie in Christ’ begins at Baptism.

In Baptism, the Lord’s disciples sacramentally die in Christ so as to live a new life.

If the disciples die in the grace Christ, physical death seals that ‘dying with Christ’, and consummates it by incorporating them fully and definitively into Christ the Redeemer.

The Church’s prayer of suffrage for the souls of the faithful departed implores eternal life not only for the disciples of Christ who have died in his peace, but for the dead whose faith is known to God (see Eucharistic prayer IV).

The just encounter God in death. He calls them to himself so as to share eternal life with them.

No one, however, can be received into God’s friendship and intimacy without having been purified of the consequences of personal sin.‘The Church gives the name purgatory to this final purification of the elect, which is entirely different from the punishment of the

damned. The Church formulated her doctrine of faith on Purgatory especially at the Councils of Florence and Trent’ (CCC,1 031).

Hence derives the pious custom of suffrage for the souls of the f aithful departed, which is an urgent supplication of God to have mercy on the souls of the dead, to purify them by the fire of His charity, and to bring to His kingdom of light and This suffrage is a cultic expression of faith in the communion of saints.

Indeed, the Church in its pilgrim members, from the earliest days of Chri stian religion, has honoured with great respect the memory of the dead; and ‘because it is a holy and wholesome thought to pray for the dead that they may be loosed from their sins’( 2 Mac 12:46) she offers her suffrages for them.

These consist primarily, in the celebration of the holy sacrifice of the Eucharist, and in other pious exercises, such as prayers for the dead, alms deeds, works of mercy, and the application of indulgences to the souls of the faithful dep arted.
