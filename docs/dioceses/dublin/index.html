Parish of the Holy Family Aughrim Street Parish Bulletin 18 October 2026 In this Sunday’s Gospel, the Pharisees once again were tr ying to trick Jesus with their question.

But his reply was sharp, to the point, yet in its directness put an end to the pharisees’ duplicity.

We can all be cunning like the Pharisees, not only towards others , but towards ourselves too.

We can focus on non-essentials, over- complicate our lives with the things that don’t matter, the things that draw us away from o ur relationship with God.

We can be mesmerised by wealth and power, even jealous of those who appear to have more than we have.

We long for material treats and lose sight of our gifts from God.

The Gospel puts us firmly back on the right road in our journey to God.

It’s a journey of simplicity and a journey in which we leave a light footprint on the world and on others.

When we strip away the non- essentials, we have a wonderful and sometimes challenging opportunity to love and be loved.

In today’s Gospel, we are invited to be less like the Pharisees and consciously reduce the number of times we try to trip others up, cut back on the rash comments and actively choose kind, uplifting words.

BUT we should probably first try to stop tripping ourselves up!

You hypocrites! Why do you set this trap for me?

TO PONDER THIS WEEK In the Gospel, the Pharisees and Herodians attempt to trap Jesus with a politically charged question: "Is it permissible to pay taxes to Caesar or not?

Instead of falling into their political snare, Jesus calls for a denarius and asks, "Whose head is this?

Whose inscription?". When they reply, "Caesar's," He delivers a timeless truth: "Give back to Caesar what belongs to Caesar—and to God what belongs to God.

While the coin carries Caesar’s likeness and currency, human beings carry the indelible image and likeness of God.

This means that while civil authority has a legitimate place, our ultimate obedience, our hearts, and our very lives belong entirely to our Creator.

This divine sovereignty is echoed in the First Reading from Isaiah 45. The Lord uses Cyrus, a pagan Persian king, as an instrument to free the exiled Israelites.

UNDERSTANDING THE MASS. The Offertory: "Blessed are you, Lord God of all Creation" What the Priest is Doing The Liturgy of the Word is complete.

Now the altar is prepared, gifts are brought forward, and the Mass moves into its second great movement - the Liturgy of the Eucharist. The priest receives bread and wine, lifts each slightly, and offers quiet prayers of blessing drawn from ancient Jewish table prayers.

He says these prayers in a quiet voice, or even silently.

These are humble gifts, bread and wine, fruit of the earth, work of human hands.

But that ordinariness is precisely the point.

God does not ask for the extraordinary. He takes what we bring and transforms it entirely.

How We Should Respond The collection taken up at this moment is not an interruption, it belongs here.

Money represents our labour, our time, our life.

Placing it in the bags is a small but genuine act of offering ourselves alongside the bread and wine.

As the gifts are brought forward, make an interior offering of your own.

Whatever you are carrying this week work, worry, joy, grief - place it consciously on that altar alongside the bread and wine.

Living This at Mass The Offertory is easily overlooked as a moment of transition.

Reclaim it. When the priest lifts the bread, lift something of yourself with it.

It is the Holy Father's annual appeal for spiritual and financial support so that the life giving work of overseas mission and missionaries can continue.

Mission Sunday collections will take place in every single parish where the Church is present.

This fund is a lifeline for struggling missionaries and the communities they serve across Africa, Asia and Latin America, where over 1,100 mission dioceses are found.

Pastors Desk A place for everything! My mother used always say - put things where they belong.

Newspapers, food, the bicycle not in the hall, and other such homely advice.

Jesus says the same here - give what belongs to where it belongs and to whom.

So Jesus is being asked about taxes and politics.

He knows he is being trapped about money.

He moves on to higher questions. Something about the answer of Jesus asks us — where do we belong?

Belonging can sound heavy - as if we are being controlled, or our money belongs to us and we can do what we like with it.

But for Jesus it's the belonging of love, not of power and control.

Much is not in our control. We are born and die at God's time.

This reminds us that we are not the masters of our lives, we come from God and go to God.

This belonging is the centre of our human family and community.

God doesn't want to control us but to love us.

But there's an addition: belonging to God means belonging to each other.

We have rights and duties of love. To give to others what belongs to others; give to the poor what belongs to the poor.

Everyone has a right to the food of the earth and the food of the mind, and shelter for the body.

It's not charity when people are given food, education, a home, freedom of religion, freedom of speech - it is justice.

To give to God what belongs to God is to share the goods of the earth!

Think of a time when you made a decision for the common good - money given away, cleaning the neighbourhood, joining a parents' group etc.

Remember and be grateful Lord, may your will that we share well the goods of the world be done.

His response is both simple and profound: “Give back to Caesar what belongs to Caesar, and to God what belongs to God.” Jesus acknowledges our responsibilities as citizens and members of society.

We have duties towards the communities in which we live and work.

Yet He reminds us that there is a deeper loyalty that comes before all others.

While coins may bear the image of Caesar, each human person bears the image of God.

The Gospel challenges us to reflect on what belongs to God in our own lives.

Our time, talents, relationships, compassion, and faith are gifts entrusted to us by the Lord.

As we fulfil our earthly responsibilities, we are also called to offer ourselves wholeheartedly to God, allowing His values of justice, truth, mercy, and love to shape everything we do.

May we live as faithful citizens of the world, while never forgetting that we belong first to God.

But here St John Bosco is encouraging us to be generous and courageous.

We may not be talking about a radical change in our lifestyle or a move to another country but it may just be that we see a gap somewhere and realise that we can fill it.

Archbishop Dermot Farrell, reminds us that one of the great blessings of Pope Francis’ ministry has been a renewed appreciation of the role of laity.

He said: “We take the place of others who have gone before, as Matthias did; filling up the ranks of soldiers, some of whom, indeed, have fought a good fight, but many of whom in every age have made void their calling.

Many are called, few are chosen.” Pope Francis said: “It is necessary to recognise “those laymen and women who feel called by virtue of their baptism to cooperate in the work of catechesis.” As Pope Benedict XVI said, the laity and clergy are “co - responsible” for sharing the good news.

It’s a task we all must take on together.

Our mission now is to reach out beyond our comfort zones and to connect to those who, as St Paul said, may never hear the word of God unless we bring it to them ourselves.
