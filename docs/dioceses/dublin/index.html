FEAST DAYS Wednes 28th October - Ss Simon & Jude Simon the Zealot , also called Simon the Cananaean, was one of the Twelve Apostles chosen by Jesus.

The title “Zealot” reflects his fervent dedication to the Jewish Law, and possibly his prior association with the Zealot movement opposing Roman rule, though this is debated.

He is distinct from Simon Peter, the “Prince of the Apostles” Jude Thaddeus , also known as Thaddaeus or Lebbaeus, was a relative of Jesus, being the brother of James the Less and a cousin of Christ. He is traditionally identified as the author of the Epistle of Jude , a short New Testament letter warning against heresy and immorality He is often called the Patron Saint of Hopeless or Desperate Causes Thur 29th Oct - St Colman Saint Colman mac Duagh 560 – 29 October 632) was born at Corker, Kiltartan , County Galway, Ireland, the son of the Irish chieftain Duac (and thus, in Irish, mac Duach ).

He initially lived as a recluse, living in prayer and prolonged fastings, first on Inismore, then in a cave at the Burren in County Clare.

With his relative, King Guaire Aidne mac Colmáin 663) of Connacht he founded the monastery of Kilmacduagh, ("the church of the son of Duac"), and governed it as abbot-bishop, not to be confused with Saint Colman, Templeshanbo 595), Ss Simon, Jude & Colman pray for us.

Parish of the Holy Family Aughrim Street Parish Bulletin 25 October 2026 In this Sunday’s Gospel, the focus is on the commandments a nd the call to love.

Jesus responds to the question about the greatest comma ndment by telling us to love God and to love our neighbour as ourselves.

These ‘commands’ to love God and those around us are not really ‘comma nds’ . Love is not love unless it is free and spontaneous.

What Jesus pro poses are not just commands or rules, but a whole approach to life and to our relat ionships with others.

Jesus gives us one ‘commandment’ consisting of two insepar able parts.

The key word is ‘love’ , but there are really three loves involved: love of God, love of others and love of self.

The love of God, the source of all being and life, comes first. Then comes, as a natural outcome, love for all those in whom God dwells and whom God creates.

Because they are the objects of his love, they must also be the objects of my love.

Lastly, there is the love of self, since I also am worthy of being loved.

Strangely enough, to implement these lovese�ectively, we may have to reverse the order to: love of self, leading to love others, leading finally to love of God.

In a way, the most basic love is love of myself.

Today’ s Gospel says: You shall love your neighbour as yourself.

On the one hand we might think this is an unnecessary co mmand.

Who doesn’t love themselves, think about themselves and worry abou t their welfare?

At the same time, we have been taught at times not to love ourselves, not to be selfish and self-centred.

The celebration of “Holywins” (“Holiness wins“), is an initiative that originated in Paris in 2002 with the aim of celebrating the eve of All Saints on 31 October in a Christian way.

Over time, this Catholic celebration has been spreading to other places in the world.

On that day, parishes and Catholic communities gather to celebrate Mass, participate in Eucharistic adoration and prayer meetings, and take part in recreational activities to encourage the participation of children, young people, and their families.

It’s customary for children to dress up as their favourite saints, to discuss the exemplary lives of the saints , and to remind children of the call to sainthood.

In addition, games are usually played, lively songs are sung, and food and sweets are shared.

So meticulous were they in keeping it, they had worked out that the biblical law contained 365 negative commandments (one ‘thou shalt not’ for every day of the year) and 248 positive commandments (one for every bone in the human body).

In addition to this written law, they believed that God had given Moses another set of laws that weren’t written down but were passed down by an unbroken chain from Moses to them.

Some Pharisees taught that every commandment was to be observed with the same attention to detail.

Others taught that the commandments ranged from serious to relatively minor.

This debate provides the background to today’s Gospel.

By asking Jesus’ opinion, his opponents are also inviting him to line up on one side or the other in the debate.

Jesus answers by quoting a verse from the Book of Deuteronomy that every member of his audience would know (Dt. 6:5).

It was part of the prayer they said twice each day, the Shema Yisrael (‘Hear , O Israel, the Lord our God is He then adds a verse that ‘resembles this’ – the command to love your neighbour as yourself (Leviticus 18:2).

On these two verses, he says, hang the whole of the Law and the Prophets, that is, God’s entire revelation to his people.

This is the law that matters. n LOVE, ONLY LOVE MT 22:34-40 We use the word ‘love’ with easy abandon today.

Think of the casual way we throw it around.

We love our bed, our favourite TV soap, our new shoes.

We love salt with our chips, sugar in our tea, and a trip to the cinema.

I love everything that’s bad for me, according to my cardiologist. Love is desire, obsession, addiction, commitment, lust, service; love is whatever we want it to be.

But if we Christians want to know what love truly is, we need only look to God.

God is love, St John tells us. God not only loves, God is love.

And where there is love, there is God. In her book Reading Genesis, Marilynne Robinson points out that this Godly love is what distinguished the God of Israel from other Middle Eastern gods of antiquity.

The Babylonians and others believed in multiple gods; Abraham and his descendants were unique in being monotheists, in believing there is but one God, who had created them in God’s very own image.

We are not images of angels or lesser gods but of the creator himself.

This was a radical way of thinking. The other gods of antiquity were remote, distant, and indifferent to the happenings here on earth.

The God of Genesis is a benign God who forgives Cain for the murder of his own brother, forgives his people time and again, and is personally invested in them, unlike the apathetic or vengeful gods that other peoples worshipped.

Israel’s God is slow to anger, rich in mercy, abiding in love.

God, Robinson says, has a lofty view of humankind, expressed poetically in Psalm 8: “What is man that you are mindful of him?Yet you have made him a little lower than the heavenly beings and crowned him with glory and honour .” God loves, and God is love.

But, when we say this, we’re not describing an up in the sky kind of love.

We’re describing the kind of concrete love that Jesus showed.

People practise this kind of love every day.

Think of parents who care for children with special needs.

Or grandparents who pick up the pieces when their child’s marriage collapses.

Or groups like the St Vincent de Paul, who help the most needy.

All of these put love into practice every day.

I like to break down the great commandment into its three parts.

We are to:* love ourselves. Healthy self-love comes from knowing I am made in God’s own image.

Healthy self-love is not the same as selfishness or narcissism.

It is to see myself the way God sees me, as one made in God’s own image.

Therefore, I am unique, lovable, irreplaceable.

Many of us find it hard to love ourselves.

Young people, influenced by social media, find it so hard to love themselves.

But if we are to love God and others, we must learn to love ourselves. love our neighbour. to love is to will the good of another.

It is to go out of our way for others. “Feed the poor .” That’s love. “Break bread with those no one will sit with.” That’s love. “Respond with patience to someone who gets on my nerves.” That’s love.

Love of neighbour means never being bystanders.

More than anything else, God will judge us on whether we loved others. love God.

God created us out of love, and sustains us lovingly each day.

We return God’s love through a life of prayer, worship and good works.

St Paul instructed the Ephesians to ‘live a life of love.

Three little words beginning with the letter L. Easy to remember.

Hard to do. That’s how we keep the great commandment. n REFLECTSAY “Lord, help me to love you and my neighbour to the best of my ability.” Pray for your parish community, that all will grow in love of God and one another.

UNDERSTANDING THE MASS. The Eucharistic Prayer: "The Heart of the Mass" What the Priest is Doing Everything in the Mass has been moving towards this moment.

The Eucharistic Prayer is the longest, most sacred prayer of the entire celebration the great act of thanksgiving through which bread and wine become the Body and Blood of Christ. The priest alone speaks it, but he does not pray alone.

He prays with the Church, through Christ, to the Father, in the Holy Spirit.

Every word carries the weight of two thousand years of faith.

At its centre comes the consecration the words of Christ at the Last Supper at which point the bread and wine are truly transformed.

How We Should Respond. This is not a moment for distraction or private prayer elsewhere.

Follow every word. The great "Amen" that concludes the Eucharistic Prayer sometimes called the Great Amen is the congregation's ratification of everything the priest has prayed . It should be the most emphatic word we speak at Mass.

Living This at Mass At the consecration, when the priest elevates the host and then the chalice, look up. ( some of you may remember the late Fr Robinson encouraging you to do so as children all those years ago! ) The sacristan rings bells precisely to call our attention.

This is the summit. Be present to it completely.

But Jesus uses that tiny piece of metal to reveal a massive spiritual truth.

He asks them, "Whose image is this, and whose inscription?

You do, because His image is stamped on your heart and soul.

In the first reading from Exodus, God reminds His people that genuine faith is expressed not only in prayer and worship but also in the way we treat others, especially those who are vulnerable.

The Israelites are instructed not to oppress the stranger, exploit the poor, or neglect those in need.

Having experienced hardship themselves, they are called to show compassion, justice and mercy to others.

In the Gospel, Jesus is asked which commandment is the greatest. His answer unites the whole of God's law into two inseparable commands: to love God with all our heart, soul and mind, and to love our neighbour as ourselves.

Love of God and love of neighbour are not separate duties but two dimensions of the one Christian calling.

These readings challenge us to examine how our faith is lived in everyday life.

It is easy to profess belief in God; it is often more demanding to show patience, kindness and understanding to those around us.

Jesus reminds us that the measure of our love for God is reflected in the care, respect and compassion we show to others.

In loving our neighbour, we reveal the presence of God's love in our world.

Jesus quotes directly from the Hebrew scriptures when he tells us that the most important commandments are:“You shall love the Lord your God with all your heart, and with all your soul, and with all your mind.” And “You shall love your neighbour as yourself.” This brief exchange is found in all three synoptic Gospels (Mark 12:28-31 and Luke 10:25- 28).

We can also say that St John’s Gospel focuses on love.

In fact, it is said that when St John was an old man he would always emphasise the importance of love.

Scripture scholars tell us that there are 613 commandments in the Hebrew Bible and there was a discussion among the rabbis as to which of them were the most important.

The words of today’s Gospel are helpful and consoling.

We are told that the entire bible can be summarised in two simple phrases which mean loving God and loving your neighbour.

In a letter to celebrate the 150th anniversary of the birth of St Therese of Lisieux, Pope Francis quoted her own words: “It is confidence and nothing but confidence that must lead us to love”. Pope Francis writes: “It is trust that brings us to love and thus sets us free from fear.

It i s trust that helps us to stop looking to ourselves and enables us to put into God’s hands what he alone can accomplish”. He concludes, “At a time of great complexity, St Therese can help us rediscover the importance of simplicity, the absolute primacy of love, trust and abandonment.” The cha llenge presented to us in today’s Gospel is not easy.

We have busy and demanding lifestyles when so many of us are juggling different work and family commitments.

Let us pray in the coming week let us ask God to help us to reach out to our neighbours with love wherever we may find them.

St Vincent de Paul once said “do whatever good lies at your hands” because he realised that sometimes those who need help may not be far away.
