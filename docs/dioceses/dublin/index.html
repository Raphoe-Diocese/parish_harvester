Monday 23rd November-St. Columban (also known as Columbanus)(born c. 543, Leinster died November 23, 615, Bobbio [Italy]; was an Irish Christian abbot and writer and one of the greatest missionaries of the Celtic Church . He initiated a revival of spirituality on the European continent.

He is venerated as the patron saint of European unity and motorcyclists and is invoked against floods . Educated in the monastery of Bangor , County Down, Columban left Ireland about 590 with 12 monks For the disciples who came to follow his rule, Columban built the nearby monasteries of Luxovium and Fontaines.

Unpopular because of his attacks on degeneracy in the Burgundian court and among local clergy , he was indicted in 603 before a synod of French bishops for keeping Easter according to the Celtic tradition (which used a different calculation from the Roman church for festival’s date).

He went with St. Gall and other monks to Switzerland, where he preached Christianity to the Germanic people.

He went to Italy and founded the monastery of Bobbio 612–614).

Columban’s works include poems, letters, and sermons.

What is the Kingdom of Christ the King? The Kingdom of God is a space.

It exists in every home where parents and children love each other.

It exists in every region and country that cares for its weak and vulnerable.

It exists in every parish that reaches out to the needy.

The Kingdom of God is a time. It happens whenever someone feeds a hungry person, or shelters a homeless person, or shows care to a neglected person.

It happens whenever we overturn an unjust law, or correct an injustice, or avert a war.

It happens whenever people join in the struggle to overcome poverty, to erase ignorance, to pass on the faith.

The Kingdom of God is in the past (in the life and work of Jesus of Nazareth); It is in the present ( in the work of the church and in the efforts of many others to create a world of goodness and justice); It is in the future (reaching its completion in the age to come).

The Kingdom of God is a condition. Its symptoms are love, justice and peace.

Jesus Christ is King! We pray today that God may free all the world to rejoice in his peace, to glory in his justice, to live in his love.

The Gospel begins with a majestic scene describing the return of the Son of Man in glory.

Jesus will end his earthly life in the humiliation of the passion and cruciğxion.

He will depart from this world unobtrusively, saying farewell to his own in the obscurity of a hillside in Galilee.

But his return will be glorious, as he comes escorted by the angels to take his seat for judgment.

Before him have gathered all the nations.

Like a shepherd at day’s end, he separates sheep from goats.

He addresses both of them. The speeches are substantially the same.

What diƬers is their reaction. The Ʈrst group consists of the sheep.

His message to them is positive: they are blessed by the Father; a place has been prepared for them in God’s kingdom since the foundation of the world.

They have passed six vital tests: they have fed the king when he was hungry, given him drink when he was thirsty, welcomed him when he was a stranger, clothed him when he was naked, and visited him when he was sick and in prison.

This comes as a surprise to the sheep. They have no memory of ever having served the king in any of these ways.

He tells them that as long as they did it to the most vulnerable of his brothers and sisters, they did it to him.

Next, he addresses those on the left. He applies the same test to them, but they fail it.

Again, they are taken aback: they have never seen the king in any kind of need.

They are told that when they failed to care for their weak brothers and sisters, they failed to care for the king.

The scene ends with the groups going their separate ways – the goats to eternal punishment and the just to eternal life.

The meaning is clear. The parables of the end times have spoken about the need to be ready for the coming of the Son of Man.

It is now unmistakable what that readiness entails.

Jesus began his teaching by proclaiming beatitudes that would mark out his disciples.

He ends his teaching with a set of six works of mercy that are a perfect match for the beatitudes.

Pope Pius XI instituted the feast in 1925 (Quas Primas) to remind a troubled world that Christ reigns in truth, justice, love and peace.

His throne is the Cross, and we serve him whenever we care for “the least of these” (Mt 25:40).

Next Sunday, 29th November, Advent begins.

Yet in our Gospel from St Matthew (25:31–46), this Shepherd-King appears as the universal Judge.

Throughout His earthly ministry, Jesus firmly rejected political power and worldly dominance, declaring that His kingdom is "not of this world". Instead, Christ reigns through supreme, self-giving love, culminating on the Cross—a Passion that St Matthew places immediately after today’s Gospel passage. his Sunday, the final Sunday of the Church’s liturgical year, we celebrate the Solemnity of Our Lord Jesus Christ, King of the Universe.

In our Mass readings, we are presented with a striking picture of God’s rule.

Our First Reading from the prophet Ezekiel portrays God as a tender Shepherd searching for His scattered sheep, while St Paul reminds us in the Second Reading that Christ’s reign is a decisive victory over death itself.

Notice the genuine surprise of both the righteous and the unrighteous in this parable.

Neither group realised they were encountering the King Himself in the poor.

This teaches us an essential truth about faith: authentic Christian charity is never calculated, performative, or self-serving.

It simply responds with compassion wherever there is human need.

As we bring our liturgical year to a close, today’s readings challenge us to examine our hearts.

Where is Christ knocking at our door today—disguised in the vulnerable, the lonely, or the struggling within our own parish and community?

When we serve them with quiet, unassuming love, we welcome the King Himself. identifies Himself completely with the hungry, the thirsty, the stranger, the naked, the sick, and the imprisoned.

UNDERSTANDING THE MASS The Pray er After Communion: "Let Us Pray" What is Happening Communion has been received.

The sacred vessels are quietly purified.

A moment of silence settles over the congregation and then the priest says once more: "Let us pray.

The Prayer After Communion is brief, almost understated.

Yet it carries something important: it asks God that what we have just received might bear fruit in our lives.

We did not simply attend a ceremony. Something happened.

The prayer acknowledges this and asks that it not stop here - that the grace of the Eucharist might continue working in us as we return to ordinary life.

How We Shou ld Respond This prayer is easily missed - we are still settling after Communion, perhaps still absorbed in personal thanksgiving.

But it deserves our attention. It is the Church's collective voice asking: "Lord, may this change us.

That is a prayer worth making our own. Not just at Mass, but in the moments after - in the car, on the walk home, over Sunday lunch.

Living This at Mass After Mass this Sunday, carry the question with you: what am I asking God to do with what I have just received?

The Eucharist is not a conclusion - it is a commissioning.

The Prayer After Communion points us forward, back into the world, changed.

Some Inspiring Words from Pope Leo The Spirit opens borders, first of all, in our hear ts.

He is the Gift that opens our lives to love.

His presence breaks down o ur hardness of heart, our narrowness of mind, our selfishness, the fears that enchain us and the narcissism that makes us think only of o urselves.

The Holy Spirit comes to challenge us, to make us confront t he possibility that our lives are shrivelling up, trapped in the vortex of individualism.

Sadly, oddly enough, in a world of burgeoning “soci al” media, we risk being ever more alone.

The Spirit of God allows us to find a new way of ap proaching and experiencing life.

He puts us in touch with our inmost self, beneath all the masks we wear.

He leads us to an encounter with the Lord by teaching us to experience the joy that is his gift.

He convinces us, that only by abiding in love, will we receive the streng th to remain faithful to his word and to let it transform us.

The Spirit also opens borders in our relationship with others . Jesus tells us that this Gift is the love between him and the Father that comes to dwell within us.

We then become capable of opening our hearts to our brothers and sisters, overcoming our rigidity, moving beyond our fear of those who are different, and mas tering the passions that stir within.

The Spirit also transforms those deeper, hidden dangers that disturb our relationships, like suspicion, prejudice or the desire to manipulate others.

The Holy Spirit, on the other hand, brings to matur ity within us the fruits that enable us to cultivate good and healthy relationships: “love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, generosity, faithfu lness, gentleness and self-control” ( Gal 5:22).

In this way, the Spirit broadens the borders of our relationships and opens us to the joy of fra ternity.

This is also a critical yardstick for the Church.

For we are truly the Church of the Risen Lord and disciples of Pentecost if there are no borders or divisions among us; if we are able to dialogue and accept one another in the Church, and to reconcile our diversi ties; and if, as Church, we become a welcoming and hospitable place for all.

He rules from the cross and his gifts to us are forgiveness, mercy, and eternal life.

The crown that Christ wears is a crown of thorns.

It’s a good challenge for us all as we celebrate the Feast of Christ the King.

Guest Speaker. What does it mean to call Jesus our King?

In a world where greatness is often measured by wealth, influence, power and success, the image of Christ as King challenges us to think differently.

Jesus did not come to demand privilege or surround himself with riches.

His crown was made of thorns, his throne was a cross, and his greatness was revealed through love, humility and service.

He shows us that true leadership is not about being served, but about serving others.

In the Gospel, Jesus reminds us that whatever we do for the hungry, the thirsty, the stranger, the sick or those in need, we do for him.

These words offer a practical challenge for everyday life.

We do not need to perform great acts or change the world overnight.

Sometimes the smallest gestures make the greatest difference: a visit to someone who is lonely, a helping hand to a neighbour, a kind word to someone who is struggling or simply taking time to listen.

Christ the King invites us to look at how we live and ask ourselves whether our actions reflect the values of his kingdom.

It is easy to speak about faith, but the real test is how we treat others.

As we bring the Church's liturgical year to a close, may we recognise Christ as our King and allow his example of love and service to guide us in the year ahead.

He doesn't say people were in prison and you visited them, or sick or naked.

He says I who am in prison, sick or naked.

It's even more than being a brother or sister of Jesus.

He identifies with each of us especially in need.

Created as we are in the image of God, Jesus sees right through us to see God.

God is the divine life of God in each person.

That makes the difference. We don't help the needy person only because he or she is needy, but because each is in the image of God and Jesus sees him or her and says'that's me.
