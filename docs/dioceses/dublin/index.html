ASHFORD & GLENEALY PARISH WEEKLY NEWSLETTER 32nd Sunday in Ordinary Time Year A 8th November 2026 RCN 20016166 Donal Neary SJ Gospel Reflections for Year A: Matthew Jesus always new The lamp is the light of love and hope, and at its centre is the light of Christ. The light is given to us in many ways in our lives - it shines within as an eternal joy, and outwards to many others.

Jesus often advises, 'keep your lamps burning' and'Let you lights shine.

Like when the bulb goes out when the electricity fails, the lamp can go out without oil.

The people of the gospel felt foolish that they had not brought oil with them to light their way.

The oil is prayer and our relationship with Jesus.

This is the essence of the Christian life.

Our Christian life is following a real, loving person: it is an invitation to get to know Jesus, and to find ourselves drawn from our hearts to follow him.

Without this living relationship with Christ, words sound empty.

We sometimes hear someone speaking of their Christian faith and cannot help wondering if it comes from the inside or is just a list of things to be believed and read.

Ignatius of Loyola's famous prayer is the prayer of the follower of Jesus; ‘Lord , teach me to know you more, love you more and serve you more faithfully in my life.' (Spiritual Exercises).

The more indicates that this is never a finished product: like love and friendship it grows in our lives.

It is exciting that Jesus is never gone from us.: risen from the dead he is always alive, always new.

Our reading of the gospel, our sharing at Mass and the sacraments and our personal prayer keep this relationship always alive, always new.

Thinking of a bright lamp, or imagining one, ask for an ever-brightening light of Christ in your life.

LAURENCE O’TOOLE (b.1128 d.1180) Lorcán Ó Tuathail Laurence was born in Castledermot, Co. Kildare in 1128. He became Abbot of Glendalough in 1153 and Archbishop of Dublin in 1162. He died in Normandy on the 14th of November 1180 and was canonised in 1225. His eventful life can be summarised under eight headings.

HOSTAGE. Laurence's father was Maurice O'Toole, King of Hy Murray.

When Laurence was 10 he was given as a hostage to Dermot McMurrough, King of Leinster, who treated him with great harshness.

The boy was sent in chains to a remote area where he was ill housed, ill-clothed and ill-fed.

For two years, the king's son learned what it was to experience real poverty and oppression. 2. MONK. After two years, Dermot was forced to release Laurence and send him to Glendalough monastery, where his father could reclaim him.

When Maurice arrived, he found his son had fallen in love with the life of the monks and he gave him permission to join the community.

Laurence was only 25 when he was elected Abbot, and he proved to be the greatest Abbot of Glendalough since St Kevin its founder.

He encouraged learning, built new churches and renewed-monastic life by bringing in monks from the continent.

He was unfailing in his care for the poor and sold the monastery treasures to feed famine victims. 3. ARCHBISHOP. In 1162 Laurence became the first Irish Archbishop of the Danish city of Dublin.

Many of the citizens were no more than nominal Christians and he made it his duty to deepen their faith and reform their lives.

He introduced Augustinian monks from France to Christ Church Cathedral to reform the liturgy and he became a member of their community.

He continued his works of charity, especially towards homeless children, whom he led and housed in his own residence.

He was tall and graceful in figure.[2] He was well known as an ascetic, wore a hair shirt, never and fasted every Friday on bread and water.

In contrast to this, it is said that when he entertained, his guests lacked for nothing while he drank water coloured to look like wine so as not to spoil the feast. He died 14th Nov 1180. Feast Day Sat next, 11th November St Laurence O’ Toole pray for us

Our final destiny is to be reunited with the Lord and with those 163 beautiful souls we remembered so deeply this week.

Reaching this heavenly goal, however, requires active preparation.

In the Gospel, Jesus warns us through the Parable of the Ten Virgins to keep our lamps lit.

The oil of faith, prayer, and quiet charity must be nurtured daily in our hearts.

May we keep our lamps trimmed, trusting that the love we shared on Monday evening is just a foretaste of our eternal home.

Tony Points to Ponder for the Thirty-Second Sunday in Ordinary Time 8 November 2026 In today’s Gospel, Jesus draws from this revelation about the future the practical moral that a Christian needs to be on the watch, living each day as if it were his last. The important thing is not to speculate about when these events will happen and what form they will take, but to live in such a way that they find us in the state of grace.

Living each day as if it were your last is a powerful concept that can help us to live more fully and with greater purpose.

It reminds us that our time on this earth is limited and that we should make the most of every moment.

It also encourages us to focus on what is truly important in life, such as our relationships with others and our relationship with God.

Keeping watch is another important aspect of this passage.

It reminds us to be vigilant and to stay alert to the signs of the times.

We should be aware of the world around us and the events that are unfolding, so that we can respond appropriately.

This means being informed about what is happening in our communities, our nation, and the world, and taking action when necessary.

Today’s Gospel, then, is a powerful reminder to Christians to live each day as if it were their last and to keep watch for the signs of the times.

By doing so, we can live more fully and with greater purpose, and we can be prepared for whatever the future may hold for us.

The Sign of Peace "Peace Be With You" What the Happening After the Our Father, the priest prays for peace and unity in the Church, then invites the congregation to offer one another a sign of peace.

This gesture has ancient roots - early Christians called it the holy kiss - and its placement here is deeply intentional.

Before we receive Christ in Communion, we are reconciled to one another.

We cannot approach the altar carrying unresolved contempt for anyone.

Christ himself taught this: “If you bring your gift to the altar and there recall that your brother/sister has something against you, leave your gift and go first to be reconciled.

How We Should Respond The Sign of Peace is not a greeting or a social moment.

It is a prayer - offered through gesture rather than words.

Offer it to those immediately around you with genuine intention, rather than reaching past others for a more comfortable exchange across the pew.

If reconciliation with someone present is genuinely needed, let this moment be its beginning.

Living This at Mass Before Mass begins, notice if you are carrying any friction or resentment towards anyone present or in your life, Let the Sign of Peace be a real moment of release - however small rather than a handshake on autopilot.

Book Review: "Loss: The New Collection" by Donna Ashworth Grief is a landscape that every one of us will have to walk through at some point in our lives.

It is the price we pay for love, yet when it arrives, it can feel incredibly isolating, confusing, and overwhelming.

For anyone currently n avigating the dark valleys of bereavement, or for those seeking the right words to comfort a friend, Donna Ashworth’s newly expan ded book, Loss: The New Collection, offers a gentle sanctuary.

Ashworth, a bestselling poet known for her deep emp athy and emotional clarity, has compiled a beautifu l Treasury of words explicitly designed to meet people in their quietes t, heaviest moments.

Rather than offering clinical advice or hollow platitudes, the book presents short, accessible poems and refle ctions that act like a warm hand to hold during the darkest days of sorrow.

One of the most profound reminders woven throughout this collection is that grief is not a problem to be solved or an illness to be cured.

Instead, Ashworth gently reveals that gre at grief is simply the continuation of great love.

She honours the unpredictability of mourning—how it comes in waves, shifting from total numbness to sharp heartbreak—a nd reassures the reader that every feeling is a normal part of the h ealing process.

For our parish community, this book serves as an in valuable resource.

It is a thoughtful gift for some one who has recently lost a spouse, parent, child, or dear friend.

Because the pieces are short and bite-sized, it is perfect for a grieving heart that may find it difficult to concentrate on a heavy, lengthy book.

Loss: The New Collection , reminds us that while losing someone casts us adrift, we do not walk the pat h to healing alone.

It is a comforting, hopeful addition to any bedside table a nd a reminder of the enduring power of love.

The light is given to us in many ways in our lives - it shines within as an eternal joy, and outwards to many others.

Jesus often advises, 'keep your lamps burning' and'Let you lights shine.

Like when the bulb goes out when the electricity fails, the lamp can go out without oil.

The people of the gospel felt foolish that they had not brought oil with them to light their way.

The oil is prayer and our relationship with Jesus.

This is the essence of the Christian life.

Our Christian life is following a real, loving person: it is an invitation to get to know Jesus, and to find ourselves drawn from our hearts to follow him.

Without this living relationship with Christ, words sound empty.

We sometimes hear someone speaking of their Christian faith and cannot help wondering if it comes from the inside or is just a list of things to be believed and read.

Ignatius of Loyola's famous prayer is the prayer of the follower of Jesus; ‘Lord , teach me to know you more, love you more and serve you more faithfully in my life.' (Spiritual Exercises).

The more indicates that this is never a finished product: like love and friendship it grows in our lives.

It is exciting that Jesus is never gone from us.: risen from the dead he is always alive, always new.

Our reading of the gospel, our sharing at Mass and the sacraments and our personal prayer keep this relationship always alive, always new.

Thinking of a bright lamp, or imagining one, ask for an ever-brightening light of Christ in your life.

CHURCH OF ST. VINCENT De Paul MARINO Thought for the day In this Sunday’s Gospel, Jesus shares the Parable of the Ten Virgins.

Five were wise and brought extra oil for their lamps; five were foolish and brought none.

When the bridegroom arrived late, the foolish virgins begged the wise for oil, but they were told there wasn't enough to share.

At first glance, the refusal of the wise virgins feels uncharitable.

But it reveals a profound spiritual truth: there are some things in life you simply cannot borrow at the last minute.

The "oil" in the parable represents our personal relationship with God.

It is built drop by drop through daily prayer, quiet moments of scripture reading, acts of hidden charity, and the frequent receiving of the Sacraments.

When we face life’s unexpected crises—or when we eventually stand before the Lord—we cannot borrow someone else’s faith, Prayer “Lord, grant me the Wisdom that seeks me out daily.

Fill my lamp with the oil of patience, charity, and deep faith, so that I may never be caught unprepared for Your arrival, but may instead step joyfully into Your eternal banquet.

The light is given to us in many ways in our lives - it shines within as an eternal joy, and outwards to many others.

Jesus often advises, 'keep your lamps burning' and'Let you lights shine.

Like when the bulb goes out when the electricity fails, the lamp can go out without oil.

The people of the gospel felt foolish that they had not brought oil with them to light their way.

The oil is prayer and our relationship with Jesus.

This is the essence of the Christian life.

Our Christian life is following a real, loving person: it is an invitation to get to know Jesus, and to find ourselves drawn from our hearts to follow him.

Without this living relationship with Christ, words sound empty.

We sometimes hear someone speaking of their Christian faith and cannot help wondering if it comes from the inside or is just a list of things to be believed and read.

Ignatius of Loyola's famous prayer is the prayer of the follower of Jesus; ‘Lord , teach me to know you more, love you more and serve you more faithfully in my life.' (Spiritual Exercises).

The more indicates that this is never a finished product: like love and friendship it grows in our lives.

It is exciting that Jesus is never gone from us.: risen from the dead he is always alive, always new.

Our reading of the gospel, our sharing at Mass and the sacraments and our personal prayer keep this relationship always alive, always new.

Thinking of a bright lamp, or imagining one, ask for an ever-brightening light of Christ in your life.
