Minors are the most vulnerable group within theglobal movements of migration. They are concrete faces, who have unique stories, and call on our conscience. Every year, millions of children and adolescents are forced to leave th eir homeland because of war, poverty, violence, environmental disasters and other tragedies. Sadly, their number continues to grow. The economic, political and military consequences of this disaster are immense. The suffering of migrant children does not end with their relocation. There are very young children who have lost parents, brothers, sisters and friends, and who have witnessed unspeakable acts of violence . Others, separated from their families for long periods, try to reunite with the m while enduring the anguish of separation. There are also minors who make the journey together with their parents, yet are exposed to ext reme and traumatic conditions. Finally, we cannot forget the tragic situation of those separated from their pa rents because of repatriation. To this already appalling situation are added further tragedies: deaths along migration routes, trafficking and exploitation, the recruitment of minors for use in armed conflicts, sexual violence, forced ma rriages, and the atrocities they have suffered or witnessed during their travels. The ir dignity is trampled upon in countless ways. In his message to mark World Day of Migrants and Refugees for 2017, Pope Francis recalled that migrant children “ in a threefold way are defenceless: they are children, they are foreigners, and they have no means to protect themselves. SUNDAY REFLECTION ON THE READINGS (Ezekiel 18:25-28; Philipians 2:1-11; Matthew 21:28-32). Today’s readings bring us face to face with oneofthe most important truths in the spiritual life: Godisnot impressed by empty words, but by convert ed lives . In the first reading, through the prophet Ezekiel, God speaks clearly: if the wicked person turns away from sin and does what is right, he will live; butifthe righteous person turns away from righteousness and does evil, he will die. The message is simple: what matters most is not where we started, but where we are going now . God is always ready to forgive, always ready to restore, always ready to give a new beginning. Nooneistoofar gone for mercy, andnoonecan boast as though they are already safe without perseverance. St. Paul in the second reading gives us the beautiful model of this new life: Je sus Christ Himself. The road of Christian greatness must include humility, se lf- emptying, and obedience. In a world where people want to be seen, promoted, praised, and admired, Christ shows us another way - thewayof service. The Gospel then drives the lesson home with the parable ofthetwo sons. One son said “No,” but later changed his mind and went. The other said “Yes,” but never went. Jesus asks the chief priests and elders a piercing question: ‘ Which one did the will of the father ?’ The answer is obvious: nottheonewho spoke nicely, but theonewho actually obeyed. This is a word forallofus. Itisnot enough tosay “Lord, Lord.” Itisnot enough to make promises in prayer, at Mass, or in public. God wants action. He wants conversion. He wants obedience that shows up in daily life - in our homes, our work, our relationships, and our moral choices. Peace be with you.
