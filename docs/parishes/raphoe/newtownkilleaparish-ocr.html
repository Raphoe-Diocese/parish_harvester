23rd August 2026 – Twenty-first Sunday of Ordinary Time
Step by Step through the Mass
The Liturgy of the Eucharist (Part 7)
The next small ritual that the priest carries out is known as the Lavabo – which derives from the
Latin word for washing. In this ritual the priest has water poured over his hands by a server or
he dips his hands into a bowl of water. This is not a hygiene ritual – though perhaps in the
distant past it may he begun life as a necessary hand washing. In the distant past (as in some
Missionary Lands to this day) not only the Bread and Wine (and offertory collection) are
brought forward at this stage of the Mass, but also many other gifts of food, oil, meat or dairy
produce are brought forward – often to be distributed later to the poor. This could be grubby
business and would have necessitated that the priest, who received these gifts, had to wash his
hands.
However, this is not the primary reason or purpose for this ritual. It takes its inspiration from
the ritual washing that the Jewish Temple Priests carried out before they offered the sacrifices
to God in the Temple. That ritual washing in the Old Testament Law of Moses was mandatory
for priests every time they would offer a sacrifice. Its meaning was clearly a physical act
seeking to communicate a spiritual reality. It was essential for maintaining the sanctity
required to approach God and perform sacred duties. The hands of a person are symbolic of
their actions – and so the washing of the hands by the priest in a sense reminded him (and those
who witnessed it) that not all his works are good, holy and sinless, since he is a mortal man and
therefore prone to sin. However, he washes his hands now to show that they are being prepared
to do something good and holy – the offering of sacrifice to God. In the Old Testament, when
the people saw a priest ritually wash his hands – they understood that a sacrifice was about to
take place. For us at the Holy Sacrifice of the Mass, when we see the priest perform this simple
ritual which could go unnoticed because it is so brief, we know that the reason for our gathering
at the Church at this Mass is now, in a sense, moving up a gear – the Sacrifice of Jesus on
Calvary is about to be made present in the here and now of our celebration of this Mass.
The priest accompanies this ritual action with a short prayer which he speaks almost inaudibly:
“Wash me, O Lord, from my iniquity and cleanse me from my sin.” It reinforces the fact that
this action is not about physical hygiene, but about being pure in heart and holy in soul –
treating the Holy Things of God in a holy way and with holy fear of the Lord. It is in some way
akin to a little last examination of conscience for the priest before he advances any further in the
offering of Holy Mass.
