On this day, we proclaim that Jesus Christ is the King of all creation, the King of our hearts, and the Lord of lives.

Yet His kingship is very diīerent from the kingship we usually associate with power, auth ority and worldly success.

Jesus does not rule from a throne of gold, surrounded by wealt h and earthly glory.

His throne is the Cross. His crown is made of thorns, and His greateste xpression of power is His willingness to love, forgive and give His life for othe rs.

He is a King who does not come to be served, but to serve; a King who does not conquer through vi olence, but through love; a King whose kingdom is built not on fear, but on mercy, truth and compassion.

In the Gospel, we see Jesus hanging on the Cross.

Above His head is wriƩen: “This is the King of the Jews.” To those who were watching, His death appeared to be a defeat.

Yet, at that very moment, Jesus revealed the true nature of His kingdom.

Even in suīering, He continued to forgive.

Even in His Įnal moments, He oīered hope to the repentant thief, promising him: “Today you will be with me in paradise.” This Gospel invites us to ask ourselves an important question: Is Jesus truly the King of my life?

It is easy to call Him our King when everything is going well , when our prayers are answered and life follows the path we have chosen.

But do we allo w Him to guide us when we face diĸculties, disappointments, uncertainty or suīering?

Do we allow His words to inŇuence our decisions, our relationships and the way we treat other people?

To recognise Christ as our King means more than simply believing in Him.

It means allowing Him to have a place in our everyday lives.

It means choosing fo rgiveness instead of resentment, kindness instead of indiīerence, honesty instead of convenience, and generosity instead of selĮshness.

It means allowing the values o f the Gospel to shape our families, our homes and our communities.

Christ's kingdom begins wherever love is stronger than hatred, where forgiveness overcomes biƩerness, where the lonely are welcomed, the poor are helped and the dignity of every human person is respected.

As we celebrate this great solemnity, let us remember that we do not simply wait for the Kingdom of God to come at the end of time.

We are called to build it here and now, through the small but meaningful choices we make every day.

May Christ be the King of our hearts, the centre of our families and foundation of our lives.

And may we never be afraid to follow Him, because His is the only kingdom that will never pass away.

b The Parables of Jesus often used parables to teach people about God, faith, love, forgiveness and the Kingdom of Heaven.

A parable is a simple story taken from everyday life, but it carries a much deeper spiritual meaning.

Jesus spoke about farmers, Įshermen, shepherds, servants, parents and ordinary people because He wanted everyone to understand that God's message is not reserved for scholars or religious experts.

It is meant for every human heart. Some of the most beautiful teachings of Jesus are found in His parables.

In the Parable of the Good Samaritan, He teaches us that our neighbour is not simply someone who lives near us or be longs to our community, but anyone who needs our compassion and help.

In the Parable of the Prodigal Son, we discover the unconditional love and mercy of God, who never stops waiting for His children to return to Him.

Through the Parable of the Sower, Jesus reminds us that God's Word is constantly being oīered to us, but it depends on the condition of our hearts whether that Word will grow and bear fruit.

The parables are not merely beautiful stories from the past. They are invitations to examine our own lives.

When we hear about the unforgiving servant, we must ask ourselves whether we are truly willing to forgive others.

When we hear about the wise and foolis h virgins, we are challenged to consider whether we are spiritually prepared to meet the Lord.

And when we hear about the talents, we are reminded that God expects us to use the gifts He has given us rather than hide them out of fear or laziness.

One important characteristic of the parables is that Jesus does not simply give us information; He challenges us to decide.

His stories invite us to look beyond appearances, question our aƫtudes and recognise what truly maƩers in life.

Sometimes their message comforts us, but at other times it challenges and even because God's Word calls us to conversion.

As we study the parables of Jesus, we should not ask only, "What did Jesus mean when He told this story?

We should also ask, "What is Jesus saying to me through this parable today?

The purpose of understanding the parables is not simply to know more about the Gospel, but to allow the Gospel to transform the way we think, speak and live.

Every parable contains an invitation: to know God more deeply, to love more sincerely and to become more faithful disciples of Jesus.

The Holy Eucharist is the heart and centre of the Catholic faith.

A ccording to the Catechism of the Catholic Church, the Eucharist is the source and summit of the Christian life (CCC 1324).

It is not simply a religious ceremony or a symbolic meal, but the greatest gift Jesus Christ has left to His Church: His own Body and Blood.

At the Last Supper, Jesus took bread and wine, blessed them, and gave them to His disciples, saying: “This is my body, which is given for you.

Do this in remembrance of me” (Luke 22:19).

Through these words, Jesus established the Eucharist and entrusted it to the Church as a lasting memorial of His sacriĮce.

The Catechism teaches that, during the celebration of the Mass, through the power of the Holy Spirit and the words of consecration, the bread and wine become the Body and Blood of Christ. This change is called transubstantiation.

Although the appearance of bread and wine re mains, Christ is truly, really and substantially present in the Eucharist (CCC 1374 – 1376).

The Eucharist is also the memorial of Christ's sacriĮce on the Cross.

Every time we celebrate Mass, we do not repeat Christ's sacriĮce but make present that one sacriĮce through which Jesus oīered Himself for salvation of the world.

At the altar, heaven and earth meet, and we are invited to participate in the love and self-giving of Christ. Receiving Holy Communion is much more than fulĮlling a religio us obligation.

It is entering into a personal relationship with Jesus, allowing Him to become part of our lives, strengthen our faith, forgive our weaknesses and transform our hearts.

The Eucharist also unites with one another, making us one body in Christ. We cannot truly receive Jes us in Communion while remaining indiīerent to the needs, suīer ing and dignity of other people.

The Catechism reminds us that we should prepare ourselves prop erly to receive Holy Communion, approaching the sacrament with faith, reverence and a heart free from grave sin.

We are also encouraged to spend time in prayer and thanksgiving after receiving the Eucharist. Finally, the Eucharist is a promise of eternal life.

Jesus gives Himself to us as the Bread of Life, nourishing us on our earthly journey and preparing us for the heavenly banquet.

The Eucharist is not simply something we receive; it is Someone we receive.

Every Mass is an invitation to encounter Jesus, to remain close to Him and to allow His presence to change the way we live, love and serve others.

Our Lord Jesus Christ, King of the Universe What is the Kingdom of God?

What is the Kingdom of Christ the King? The Kingdom of God is a space.

It exists in every home where parents and children love each other.

It exists in every region and country that cares for its weak and vulnerable.

It exists in every parish that reaches out to the needy.

The Kingdom of God is a time. It happens whenever someone feeds a hungry person, or shelters a homeless person, or shows care to a neglected person.

It happens whenever we overturn an unjust law , or correct an injustice, or avert a war.

It happens whenever people join in the struggle to overcome poverty, to erase ignorance, to pass on the Faith.

The Kingdom of God is in the past (in the life and work of Jesus of Nazareth); it is in the present (in the work of the Church and in t he eīorts of many others to create a world of goodness and justice); it is in the future (reaching its completion in the age to come).

The Kingdom of God is a condition. Its symptoms are love, justice, and peace.

Jesus Christ is king! We pray today that God may free all the world to rejoice in his peace, to glory in his justice, to live in his love.

Christ the King The solemnity of Christ the King marks the end of the Church’s liturgical year.

This feast was instituted by Pope Pius XI in 1925 to promote devotion to the Universal Lordship of Christ in response to the growing secularism of the Western world.

In 1969, Pope Paul VI gave it a new title, ‘Jesus Christ, King of the Universe’ and raised its status from a feast to a ‘Solemnity.

At a time when many people have scant regard for monarchs or kings, we need to ask ourselves what does it mean to honour Jesus Christ as King?

What kind of kingship are we celebrating?

The Holy Trinity – A Model of Family Love The mystery of the Holy Trinity teaches us something beautiful and essen tial about family life: love is not simply something we feel; it is something we give, receive and share.

God is Father, Son and Holy Spirit – three Persons in one God, united in perfect love, c ommunion and self-giving.

Although we can never fully understand this mystery, it oīers Christian families a wonderful model for how to live together.

In the Trinity, no Person lives for Himself.

The Father loves t he Son, the Son loves the Father, and the Holy Spirit is the bond of love between them.

Their relationship is not based on competition, selĮshness or the desire to dominate, but on complete trust, respect and mutual giving.

This is the kind of love that every Christian family is called to reŇect.

Family life is one of the Įrst places where children discover what love really means.

They learn not only from what their paren ts tell them, but especially from what they see every day.

When children witn ess their parents forgiving one another, speaking with kindness, h elping each other and making sacriĮces for the good of the family, they begin to understand the love of God.

Of course, no family is perfect. There are moments of disagreement, tire dness, misunderstanding and impatience.

Parents sometimes make mistakes, and children do not always behave as they should.

But a Christian family is not a family without problems; it is a family that continually chooses to love despite its diĸculties.

Saying “I am sorr oīering forgiveness, listening patiently and making peace are small but powerful ways of reŇecting the love of the Holy Trinity.

The Holy Trinity also reminds us that unity does not mean that be the same.

Each family member has diīerent gifts, talen ts, personalities and needs.

Children should be encouraged to discov er and develop their individuality while learning to respec t others.

A loving family is a place where everyone feels accepted, valued and importan t.

Parents have a special responsibility to make God present in thei r homes.

Through family prayer, Sunday Mass, reading the Gospel to gether and simple acts of kindness, they help their children discover that God is not distant but lives among those who love one another.

Perhaps the most important lesson of the Holy Trinity for every family is this: a home becomes a reŇection of God when love is at its centre.

When family members learn to give rather than demand, forgi ve rather than blame, and serve rather than seek aƩention, their home becomes a place where God's presence can truly be experienced.

Our families may never be perfect, but with God's grace, they can become schools of love, unity and faith.

And through the love we share at home, our children can begin to discover the greatest truth of all: Go d Himself is love.
