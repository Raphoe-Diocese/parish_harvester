For the most part in this new liturgical year, we will listen to the Gospel of St Mark.

While Mark’s Gospel is presented in the Bible as the second of the four Gospels that we hear (Matthew, Mark, Luke & John), it is believed to have been the first written (probably be tween 50 – 70 AD), and directed to the needs of early Christian communities, particularly the Gent It is the shortest of the Gospels, with just 16 chapters.

Jesus is pictured by Mark as a man of action and authority.

He puts emphasis on what Jesus did, rather than on His words.

Mark was not one of the apos tles chosen by Jesus, but he was a companion of Peter (Simon Peter) and much of what is written by Mar k draws from the preaching and eyewitness testimony of Peter, who had accompanied Jesus during His publ ic ministry.

Most importantly, Mark’s Gospel gives us a clear p icture of what it means to be an authentic disciple or follower of Jesus.

As disciples today, we are called to follow the example of Jesus.

We pray that, in this coming liturgical year, as we listen to Gospel of St Mark, the Holy Spirit willg uide us in following Christ’s message in our daily lives, in all that we do and say.‘And at once they left their nets and followed him. ’ Mark 1:18St Mark, pray for us.

SUNDAY 22 NOVEMBER 2026 Gospel Reflection Matthew 25:31–46 In the gospel today, Jesus gives us a powerful and moving picture of the final judgment.

The Son of Man returns in glory and separates the sheep from the goats, not according to wealth, success, or even outward religious practices, but according to how they responded to those in need.

Jesus identifies Himself with the hungry, thirs ty, the stranger, the sick, and those imprisoned, teaching us that whenever we show love and compassion to others, we are serving Him.

This Gospel reminds us that faith is meant to be lived.

It is easy to say that we love God, but Jesus invites us to make that love visible through acts of kindness, mercy, and generosity.

The sheep are prai sed because their care for others flowed naturally from hearts shaped by God's love.

The challenge for us is not simply to avoid doing wrong, but to actively seek opportunities to do good.

Every person in need is an opportunity to encounter Christ and to share His love with the world.

No act of kindness is ever too small in God's eyes.

A listening ear, an encouraging word, a helping hand, or a generous gift can become a sign of God's presence in someone's life.

Let us ask the Lord to give us eyes that see the suffering around us, hearts fill ed with compassion, and hands ready to serve.

As we follow the example of Jesus, who came not to be served but to serve, may we bring His love to those we meet each day.

And when we stand before Him, may we hear those joyful words we all long to hear: "Well done, good and faithful servant" (Matthew 25:21).

May the Lord’s grace guide us, His peace sustain us, and His blessing remain with us always.

They work alongside local people, discovering individuals and projects they think deserve a share of their fortune, before coming clean and surprising them with monetary gifts to help them in their work, Often those who most impress the benefactor are those who are quietly working for the benefit of others, who give generously of their time and talents to make a better life for those in their local community.

They are the ones who, in spite of their busy lives, are also the most welcoming and helpful to the undercover millionaire.

They are not in it for the money. They are not generous to the visitor among them because he is rich or because they expect to get something out of them.

They are the people who see a need and do something to tackle it, and their simple acts of generosity lead to great rewards.

Jesus expects us to treat everyone we encounter with sensitivity and generosity.

There are simple acts of mercy we can carry out each day.

Such love for others is love for God: as the Gospel tells us,‘In so far as you did this to the least of these brothers of mine, you did it to me.

Feasts during the Week: Monday - St. Columban: (also known as Columbanus) was born around 453. He became a monk of Bangor and later principal teacher there.

In 591, desiring to ‘go on pilgrimage for Christ’, he set out with twelve companions and came to Burgundy.

He established monasteries at Annegray, Lexeuil and Fontaine according to Irish rule.

Later he founded Bregenz in Austria and his greatest foundation at Bobbio, near Genoa, where he died in 615. He is remembered as one the greatest of the Irish missionary monks.

FEAST OF CHRIST THE KING We have been fit for the kingdom by Jesus’s suffering death and resurrection.

Like Jesus, we are kingdom kinds of people.

And that means that we have the opportunity, through the power of Christ’s cross, to hold together the tensions that life presents to us There are plenty of them to be found.

Maybe we aren’t surrounded by rulers and soldiers and criminals; maybe it’s frustrating neighbours and family members with odious political views and classmates who come from different backgrounds.

But no matter what hostilities or differences are swirling around us, we, the power of the cross of Christ and not on our own power, can stand in the gap and make peace between parties.

Jesus, after all, was able to make peace with his murderers, asking God to forgive them.

And as we saw in today’s reading, Jesus promised someone facing death by execution that he would remember him in paradise.

In God’s kingdom, no one is forgotten. The cross is strong enough to absorb all the world’s contradictions and make peace between them.

We can bring all our inner contradictions to Christ on the Cross.

May we, who have been seduced by the mysteries of the cross, follow Jesus and become a sign of peace in our parish and in a world beset by hostilities, misunderstandings, and resentments.

The Son of Man returns in glory and separates the sheep from the goats, not according to wealth, success, or even outward religious practices, but according to how they responded to those in need.

Jesus identifies Himself with the hungry, thirsty, the stranger, the sick, and those imprisoned, teaching us that whenever we show love and compassion to others, we are serving Him.

This Gospel reminds us that faith is meant to be lived.

It is easy to say that we love God, but Jesus invites us to make that love visible through acts of kindness, mercy, and generosity.

The sheep are praised because their care for others flowed naturally from hearts shaped by God's love.

The challenge for us is not simply to avoid doing wrong, but to actively seek opportunities to do good.

Every person in need is an opportunity to encounter Christ and to share His love with the world.

No act of kindness is ever too small in God's eyes.

A listening ear, an encouraging word, a helping hand, or a generous gift can become a sign of God's presence in someone's life.

Let us ask the Lord to give us eyes that see the suffering around us, hearts filled with compassion, and hands ready to serve.

As we follow the example of Jesus, who came not to be served but to serve, may we bring His love to those we meet each day.

And when we stand before Him, may we hear those joyful words we all long to hear: "Well done, good and faithful servant" (Matthew 25:21).

May the Lord’s grace guide us, His peace sustain us, and His blessing remain with us always.

What is the Kingdom of Christ the King? The Kingdom of God is a space.

It exists in every where parents and children love each other.

It exists in every region and country that c ares for its weak and vulnerable.

Sunday 22nd November 2026 Thirty-Fourth Sunday in Ordinary Time The Solemnity of Christ the King marks the end of the Church’s liturgical year.

At a time when many people have scant regard for monarchs or kings, we need to ask ourselves what does it mean to honour Jesus Christ as king?

What kind of kingship are we celebrating?

Yes, Jesus is a king but, as he himself states clearly, his kingdom is ‘not of this world’ (cf.

Jn 18:36). He has no stately palace, no mighty army, no marble throne.

He resides among the needy and the vulnerable, the hungry, the naked, the thirsty, the stranger, the sick and those in prison.

The king we are called to worship and serve, as today’s gospel reading from Matthew makes clear, is a king who identifies himself with the most neglected of God’s children.

And the way he wants to be served is by practical care and concern for these brothers and sisters of ours: ‘I was hungry and you gave me food.

I was thirsty and you gave me drink; I was a stranger and you made me welcome; naked and you clothed me, sick and you visited me, in prison and you came to see me’(Mt 25:35-36).

The challenge of today’s gospel is to recognise and respond to Christ in the broken and wounded people of our world.

Christ is still suffering in those who are hungry for food and for justice; in the strangers longing for acceptance and a word of welcome; in the sick needing healing and care; and in the prisoners hoping for a friendly smile and a listening ear.

Looking out for and responding to such people is to recognise and attend to the broken body of Christ.
