b The Gospel According to John holds a special place among the four Gospels.

While MaƩhew, Mark and Luke are often called the Synoptic Gospels, John oīers us a deeper theological and sp iritual reŇection on the identity of Jesus Christ. His Gospel invites us not simply to learn about Jesus, but to encounter Him personally, believe in Him and discover the meaning of life through Him.

John begins with one of the most beautiful and profound statements in the Bible: “In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God” (John 1:1).

With these words, John reveals that Jesus is not simply a great teacher or prophet, but the eternal Son of God, who became human and came to live among us.

The central message of the Gospel is that God loves humanity so deeply that He gave us His only Son so that we might have eternal life.

One of the distinctive features of John's Gospel is the use of signs and miracles.

These are not simply extraordinary events; they reveal who Jesus truly is.

The changing of water into wine at Cana, the healing of the blind man, the multiplication of loaves and the raising of Lazarus all point towards Jesus as the source of life, hope and salvation.

Another important theme is light and darkness.

Jesus declares: “I am the light of the world.

Whoever follows me will never walk in darkness but will have the light of life” (John 8:12).

Through this image, John reminds us that faith in Christ helps us Įnd direction in moments of confusion , suīering and uncertainty . John also places great emphasis on love.

At the Last Supper, Jesus gives His disciples a new commandment: “Love one another as I have loved you” (John 13:34).

This is the heart of Christian discipleship.

To follow Jesus means to love generously, forgive sincerely, serve humbly and recognise His presence in other people.

Perhaps the most important purpose o f this Gospel is expressed near its conclusion: “These are wriƩen so that you may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, and that believing you may have life through his name” (John 20:31).

For us today, the Gospel according to John is an invitation to deep en our relationship with Jesus.

It challenges us to move beyond knowing about Him towards truly knowing Him, trusting His word and allowing His love to transform our daily lives.

The more deeply we encounter Christ, the more clearly, we discover who God is and who we are called to become.

Baptism – The Beginning of New Life in Christ According to the Catechism of the Catholic Church, Baptism is the Įrst and most important of the seven sacraments because it is the gateway to life in the Spirit and the door through which we enter the Church.

Throug h Baptism, we are freed from sin, reborn as children of God, and be come members of the Body of Christ. The Catechism teaches that Baptism is no t simply a religious ceremony or a family tradition.

It is a profound enco unter with God's grace.

Through the water and the Holy Spirit, we receive a new life in Christ. As Jesus tells Nicodemus in the Gospel of John: “Unless one is born of water and the Spirit, he cannot enter the kingdom of God” (John 3:5).

Baptism – Freedom from Sin and New Life One of the central teach ings of the Catechism is that Baptism washes away all sin, including original sin, and makes us a new creation in Christ. We are no longer separated from God but become His beloved sons and daughters.

Through this sacrament, we receive the gift of the Holy Spirit and are welcomed into the family of the Church.

Baptism also gives us a new identity. We belong to Chris t, and our lives are called to reŇect His love, mercy and goodness.

It is simply something that happened to us on the day of our baptism; it is a gift and a responsibility that should shape our entire Christian journey.

Baptism – A Lifelong Commitment The Catechism reminds us that Baptism is the foundation of the whole Christian life.

It calls us to live according to the Gospel, to rejec t sin, to forgive others, to love our neighbours and to remain faithful to God . For parents and godparents, the baptism of a child carries a special responsibility.

They are called to help the child grow in faith, introduce them to prayer, teach them Christian values and show them through their own example what it means to follow Jesus.

We should also remember our own baptism.

Do we live as people who belong to Christ?

Does our faith inŇuence our decisions, our relationships and the way we treat others?

Ultimately, Baptism is not only about the beginning of our Christian life; it is about living that life every day.

It reminds us that we are loved by God, called to holiness and invited to walk with Christ towards eternal life. “Baptism is the basis of the whole Christian life, the gateway to life in the Spirit.” (CCC 1213) Exploring the Introductory Rites 1 The procession of the priest celebrant and ministers through the body of people is a visible reminder to us that we gather as the Body of Christ. Christ is with us, and we stand together as a sign of welcome and reverence for his presence among us.

Our hym n unites us in one voice of praise.

The priest reverences the alt ar with a bow and a kiss as the altar is a symbol of Christ at the heart of this assembly.

GREETING: All make the sign of the Cross, and the priest celebrant greets us from the Chair in words taken from Scripture.

These words remind us that in our gathering, the God of the Trinity, the God of grace, love and peace, is with us and working upon us through this celebration.

PENITENTIAL ACT: Before we enter deeper into the Mass, we take one more moment to prepare ourselves, using one of three prayer options given to us in the Missal.

Conscious that we all likely come with things we would like to change in our lives, behaviou rs we would like to be diīerent, in the ConĮteor we acknowledge before our brothers and sisters that we have sinned, and we ask for their prayers.

But even greater than our sin, we trust in the enduring mercy and love of God, and so we can pray in conĮdence, Lord, have mercy.

On some Sundays, especially in Easter Time, in place of the usual Penitential Act, the blessing and sprinkling of water to recall our baptism may take place.

Points to Ponder In today’s Gospel, Jesus draws from this revelation about the future the practical moral that a Christian needs to be on the watch, living each day as if it were his last. The important thing is not to speculate about when these events will happen and what form they will take, but to live in such a way that theyĮnd us in the state of grace.

Living each day as if it were your last is a powerful concept that c an help us to live more fully and with greater purpose.

It reminds us that our time on this earth is limited and that we should make the most of every moment.

It also encourages us to focus on what is truly important in life, such as our relationships with others and our relationship with God.

Keeping watch is another important aspect of this passage.

It to be vigilant and to stay alert to the signs of the times.

Sr. Niamh Galvin O. P . Intercom Nov. 2026 p.23St Mary’s Parish Novena of Masses for the Holy Souls 2026. 22nd November – 30 November 2026 Praying With The Dying.

The Irish prayers for the dying are among the oldest and most beautiful in the tradition.

They are not prayers asking God to reconsider.

They are prayers of accompaniment – of walking with the dying person to the edge of what can be walked to.‘Go dtuga Dia suaimhneas dó’ – may God give him peace. ‘Ná biodh eagla ort’ – do not be afraid. ‘Támé ag gui ar do shon’ – I am praying for you.

The Irish prayers at the deathbed are spoken aloud, in the presence of the dying, to the dying – not over their heads in a separate conversation with God but directly, personally, the living speaking to the barely living across the distance that is opening between them.

The tradition understood that hearing is the last sense to go, and the prayers were spoken accordingly.

The rosary beads wrapped through the fingers of the dying were not placed there as a formality.

They were placed there as an instrument – the familiar decades of the rosary being said by the gathered family throughout the dying hours, the repetition of the familiar prayers a thread that the dying person could follow through the darkness if they could still hear, could still hold, could still find in the sound of the voices around them the community that had held them their entire life.

If children are present they are learning something that no school teaches – how the Irish meet their dead.

How to be present without flinching. How to pray without preforming.

How to love someone through the last thing that can be done for them, which is to be in the rom and say the words and hold the hand.

This is the tradition. This is what it looks like from the inside.
