Twenty-Eighth Sunday in Ordinary Time The landowner had prepared for a large crowd.

When he sent his servants out to personally remind the guests to come, some of the servants were mistreated, beaten and even killed by his prospe ctive guests!

Quite naturally, the king was outraged. What does this Sunday’s Gospel say to us?

I doubt that we have had the experience this story speaks of.

However, we all have been disappointed and upset at various times in our lives.

We may have been deeply hurt by someone or betrayed by another.

Our natural human response is to be angry, hurt and disappointed – and rightly so.

Hopefully, we haven’t decided to kill anyone because of that!

In these situations, we need to take time to calm down and deal with the hurt, anger and disappointment.

We need to acknowledge and feel the emotions.

If we repress them, most likely the emotions will come back with a vengeance . But we can’t stop there.

We also need to honestly acknowledge what we are experiencing but then with time to gradually ‘let these feelings go’ and forgive.

Most often this is the last thing that we want to do!

It will take time but it is an important step to take.

If we nurse our anger and hurt, we are not hurting the individuals who disappointed us, but we are hurting ourselves and those we love!

If we are not able to forgive, we simply need to ask God to help us.

May we have the grace to ask God to heal the anger and hurt – and to free us!

That honour falls to pope Leo XIV. The summer of 2026 was bookended by two hugely significant Papal visits in Europe.

In June Pope Leo visited Spain and then in September he visited France.

In total almost 3 million people came out to greet him across both visits Pope Leo has a distinctively different style of papacy than his predecessor Pope Francis.

Inherently shy, Leo seems to be more considered in what he says and does.

In many ways in our fractured world he has become a global voice of unity and reason.

Pope Leo hasn’t been slow to condemn the growth of Christian nationalism, while acknowledging the benefits of he has cautioned its unrestricted growth and in his condemnation of war he has been unequivocable in his defence of the sanctity of all human life.

On every Papal visit he has made a point of not only meeting with victims of clerical abuse but also with migrants – it was telling that he declined an invitation to his native America to be part of its 250th Anniversary celebrations choosing instead to visit, on July 4th, the Italian island of Lampedusa to highlight the plight of migrants and refugees crossing the Mediterranean.

But none of that would have hinted at the enormous crowds which came our to greet and pray with Pope Leo on his visits to Spain and to France.

In Spain, for example, some 600,000 young people joined Pope Leo for an open air prayer vigil which culminated in silent Adoration of the Blessed Sacrament while almost 1 million people attended Mass on the Plaza de Cibeles, in central Madrid.

Last week in Paris some 300,000 people lined the streets to greet Pope Leo as he made his way to Notre Dame and over 80,000 young people packed into the Stad de France that night for an event which included a reenactment of the Catholic history of France and which concluded with silent veneration of a relic of the true cross.

The following day Pope Leo visited Lourdes where joining the sick he walked in the candle lit procession and celebrated Mass for 150,000 pilgrims.
