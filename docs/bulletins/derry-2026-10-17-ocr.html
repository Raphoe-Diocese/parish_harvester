The Deep End - A real royal wedding by Jane Mellett Today’s Gospel is difficult.
Stories of judgement, weeping and gnashing of teeth just don’t appeal much to most people.
Failure to turn up to a dinner party was of course bad manners.
To refuse an invite to a royal wedding was even more serious.
To kill the servants or messengers who came to you with the invite…well this is pushing things a bitfar.
As with all parables, there is more to this than meets the eye.
God wants to be in a relationship with each of us.
We are all invited to his banquet. We exclude ourselves from being in fellowship with him, from his hospitality, when we give God the brush-off.
We sometimes do our best to put ourselves outside of his presence.
So what host would not be offended? And what about that poor chap who did come but was not suitably dressed?
If we accept the invitation we should be ready for change and not just change of an outer garment.
The issue for Matthew is the replacement of the unworthy in the community with those who have responded to Jesus’ demands for his Kingdom.
And as usual, they are not the people you would expect!
POINTS TO PONDER Complexity and perplexity Critics of Pope Francis have consistently maintained that Pope Francis generated confusion in the Church, whether t hat be at the individual level – or at the global level – the Synod and the wider synodal process.
I don’t disagree that there is confusion, but I don’t think Pope Francis can be blamed for it.
Confusion is an unavoid able consequence of a time when unity is needed but uniformity is no longer possible.
Pope Francis did not invent this plurality; he made it impossible to ignore.
The Church is no longer able, if indeed it ever was, to sustain communion by assuming cultural uniformity of a single European grammar for articulating the Gospel.
It is a universal Church of diverse peoples with languages and histories, rites and cultures, joys and wounds all of their own.
This is precisely the ecclesiological horizon affirm ed by the Synod’s Final Document , which insists that ‘the harmony created by the Spirit is not uniformity’ and that ‘the unity of the Church is not uniformity, but an organic blending of legitimate diversities.
The Final Document also describes the Catholic Church as a ‘Church of Churches’ and sees synodal renewal as fos tering ‘an appreciation of local contexts as the place where universal call from God manifests and fulfils itself . Taking legitimate diversity seriously may mean introducing a measure of complexity and even perplexity into the Chur ch.

The Invitation of Grace Matthew 22:1–14 In today's Gospel, Jesus compares the Kingdom of Heaven to a wedding feast. Everything has been prepared by the king, yet those first invited refuse to come, preoccupied with their own affairs.
Others are invited, both good and bad, reminding us that God's love is a gift, not something we earn.
But what about the guest without a wedding garment?
The garment represents a life transformed by God's grace, particularly through love and charity.
There are two dangers in our spiritual lives: believing we must make ourselves worthy of God's invitation, or accepting that invitation without allowing His love to change us.
Christian holiness is neither striving to prove ourselves nor remaining unchanged.
It is surrendering to Christ and allowing His grace to work within us.
We do not earn our place at God's table.
We receive His invitation and allow His love to transform us.

A short reflection on today’s Gospel Matthew 21:1-14 In today’s Gospel, Jesus compares the Kingdom of Heaven to a wedding banquet prepared by a king.
The invitation is generous and freely offered, yet many of those invited choose not to come.
Eventually, the invitation is extended to everyone - to people from the roads and crossroads, to those who might never have expected a place at the table.
This is a beautiful reminder of the generosity of God.
His invitation is not reserved for a select few.
God calls each of us into friendship with him and offers us a place in the joy of his Kingdom.
We do not earn this invitation; it is a gift of grace.
Yet the Gospel also reminds us that accepting God’s invitation calls for a response.
The wedding garment can be understood as a sign of a heart that has been changed by God's love - a life clothed in faith, compassion, forgiveness and charity.
It is not enough simply to hear God's invitation; we are called to let it shape the way we live.
Perhaps there are times when we are like those who were too busy or distracted to accept the invitation.
The pressures and concerns of daily life can easily push God to the margins.
This Gospel invites us to pause, recognise the grace being offered to us, and respond with open and grateful hearts.
As a parish community, may we remember that there is always room at God's table.
May we welcome others with the same generosity that God shows us, and may our lives bear witness to the joy of accepting his invitation.

When everything is ready for the feast, the king sends his servants, not once but twice, to call the invited guests to come.
When these refuse to come, for one reason or another, he finally asks his servants to go to the crossroads in the town and invite everyone they can find, good and bad alike, to come to the wedding.
These come, and the ‘wedding hall is filled’ (Mt 22:10). Like most of the parables of Jesus, this is a story about the Kingdom of God.
All are called to be part of the kingdom, but not everyone appreciates the invitation, or responds to it appropriately.
Sadly, despite repeated invitations, they refuse to come.
Instead, they seize the servants, mistreat and kill them.
This refers to the hostile reception given to the prophets.
All are invited, good and bad alike, until the wedding hall is filled.
What Jesus is saying is that everyone is invited to the Kingdom of God.
All kinds of people are called to be part of God’s new world.
It does not belong to an elite group of specially chosen ones.
It encompasses saints and sinners. The only requirement is a ‘wedding garment’ (Mt 22:11-12). But what is this wedding garment?
St Paul gives us the answer: “You should be clothed in sincere compassion, in kindness and humility, gentleness and patience….
