Several years ago, an American woman called Tamara Fowler was due to get married.

Two hundred guests were invited. But then, a few weeks before the Tamara decided to break off her engagement.

Her parents were heartbroken. They had been looking forward to the wedding and had spent thousands of dollars planning a dinner reception at a top restaurant.

Her father thought about the situation, talked it over with his wife, and prayed about it one night before going to bed.

The next morning, he later said, he knew what they had to do.

They decided to go ahead with the wedding dinner as planned—but not with the original guest list. Instead, they invited a different group: 200 homeless men, women and children.

The family contacted a local homeless charity.

The woman who picked up the phone later said she was so shocked, she thought it was a prank call.

The Fowlers explained what they wanted to do and that they especially wanted to help children.

And so, on the day the wedding was due to take place, buses brought dozens of needy people to one of Atlanta’s top wedding venues.

Children had space outside to play. Face painters and jugglers were hired to entertain them.

The children, it transpired, had to be shown how to eat hors’ d’oeuvres, how to use the right cutlery.

They weren’t used to fine china. At evening’s end, the caterer was amazed.

For the first time, she said, at the end of a big wedding there were no leftovers.

Every plate was clean. Those special guests were fed, but they received more than a great meal that day – they received love.

They felt included. They hadn’t been forgotten.

That meal was a foretaste of the kingdom of heaven, an image of the heavenly feast. Pope Francis often described the church as a big tent and not a small chapel that holds only a few.

He repeatedly stressed the importance of welcome and inclusion.

Inclusion reflects God’s mercy , he said, and God’s mercy embraces everyone.

Christians must mirror God’s mercy by opening our hearts to others without judging or excluding anyone based on status, race, ability, or background.

Inclusion, he emphasised, must go beyond rhetoric and be reflected in daily life.

It should create a “culture of social tenderness.” Days after his election, Pope Leo said the same: “We must be a church with open arms for everyone, open to all who need our presence, dialogue, love.” As they sat in that hotel eating great food off of fine china, those homeless people must have said to themselves, “I wish it could be like this all the time.” Of course, it couldn’t.

But they had been given a glimpse of how things could be, of how things should be. to be Christian is to welcome and include.

It’s the opposite of them and us. He has sent out the invitations, but those he invited to the feast aren’t interested.

They don’t show up. They make excuses and avoid committing themselves.

The king is furious and tells his servants that those he invited have shown themselves unworthy of the banquet.

Instead, the servants are to go out onto the roads and streets and invite absolutely everyone they can find.

There is no need for personal invitations.

Everyone is welcome. Before long, the wedding hall is full.

In the parable, the king represents God.

The great feast is a traditional Jewish image of God’s kingdom.

We see this in today’s first reading from Isaiah.

Matthew is telling the story of God’s involvement with God’s people, and how this is played out in the life of Jesus.

Matthew knows the end of the story. He has seen how Jesus was rejected by the people of Israel, for whom he came, just as they had rejected the prophets before him.

They have rejected God’s invitation over and over again, and so the Good News is now preached to the Gentiles.

God is opening the gates of the kingdom to everyone everywhere.

God is a gracious host who hopes that everyone will come to his feast. It doesn’t matter who we are or where we come from.

All that is asked of us is that we respond wholeheartedly to this invitation by doing our best to live in tune with the values of God’s kingdom. n SAY “Lord, help me to live in such a way that I will be able to feast at your heavenly banquet forever.

The Mass is therefore the greatest prayer we have.

Because of this, the Church has always encouraged the faithful to ask that Mass be offered for particular intentions; for example, for the repose of the soul of a deceased loved one, for someone’s recovery from illness, for a wedding anniversary, or for some other person or particular need.

So, when you request a Mass for your deceased loved one, you are essentially saying: “I want the Church to offer the Holy Sacrifice of the Mass for them. ” When you request a Mass for someone who is sick, you are saying: “I want the Holy Sacrifice of the Mass to be offered for this person’s healing and needs. ” When you request a Mass in thanksgiving, you are saying: “I want to join my thanksgiving to Christ’s perfect offering of Himself to the Father. ” The Mass intention does not add some extra power to the Mass.

The Mass is already the infinite sacrifice of Christ. Rather, the Church allows us to direct the fruits and prayers of a particular celebration of the Mass toward a particular intention.

Sending a Mass card is a statement that we have arranged to have Mass offered for that person’s intentions.

It is a beautiful expression of our love and concern for the person concerned.
