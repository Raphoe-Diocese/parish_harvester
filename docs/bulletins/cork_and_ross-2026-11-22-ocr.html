Naomh Ciarán of Cape Clear The tradition in West Cork is that St Patrick never came here, instead we were converted by Naomh Ciarán.

Devotion to him is particularly strong on Cape Clear Island, where he is said to have been born in the late 300s.

The written accounts of his life date from the 1200s or later.

They tell us that he studied on the continent and, after meeting St Patrick there, he returned to Ireland to begin his mission.

He founded his first church on Cape Clear and later moved to Ossory, where his father came from.

He is credited as being Ireland’s first saint.

There are numerous stories about him, many recalling his great love of animals.

Others are more fantastical, including that he rose the dead and that he lived to be over 200 years old.

In Cornwall, where he is known as St Piran, it is believed that he floated there on a millstone and converted the Cornish people.

It is difficult to know what is true about Naomh Ciarán, but tradition and other evidence, such as the links between Fifth-century Ireland and Britain, mean we should not dismiss his claim to the title of Ireland’s first saint.

William Casey Saint of the Week St Columban (Columbanus) 543–615) was an Irish missionary and abbot who played a vital role in reviving Christianity across medieval Europe.

Born in Leinster he trained at Bangor Abbey in Ireland which was a great centre of learning.

He travelled with twelve companions to Gaul, establishing monasteries in France, Switzerland, and Italy, including Luxeuil-les-Bains and Bobbio.

Renowned for his strict monastic rule, scholarship, and defence of Irish liturgical traditions, he died in Bobbio, leaving a lasting spiritual and cultural legacy.

Naomh Ciarán of Cape Clear The tradition in West Cork is that St Patrick never came here, instead we were converted by Naomh Ciarán.

Devotion to him is particularly strong on Cape Clear Island, where he is said to have been born in the late 300s.

The written accounts of his life date from the 1200s or later.

They tell us that he studied on the continent and, after meeting St Patrick there, he returned to Ireland to begin his mission.

He founded his first church on Cape Clear and later moved to Ossory, where his father came from.

He is credited as being Ireland’s first saint.

There are numerous stories about him, many recalling his great love of animals.

Others are more fantastical, including that he rose the dead and that he lived to be over 200 years old.

In Cornwall, where he is known as St Piran, it is believed that he floated there on a millstone and converted the Cornish people.

It is difficult to know what is true about Naomh Ciarán, but tradition and other evidence, such as the links between Fifth-century Ireland and Britain, mean we should not dismiss his claim to the title of Ireland’s first saint.

William Casey Saint of the Week St Columban (Columbanus) 543–615) was an Irish missionary and abbot who played a vital role in reviving Christianity across medieval Europe.

Born in Leinster he trained at Bangor Abbey in Ireland which was a great centre of learning.

He travelled with twelve companions to Gaul, establishing monasteries in France, Switzerland, and Italy, including Luxeuil-les-Bains and Bobbio.

Renowned for his strict monastic rule, scholarship, and defence of Irish liturgical traditions, he died in Bobbio, leaving a lasting spiritual and cultural legacy.

Sunday 22nd November 2026 Feast of Christ the King ~ Year A Gospel Reflection We Will Be Judged by Love Alone On the final Sunday of our liturgical year, the Gospel speaks to us of endings —and of judgement.

We know that, at the end of time, each of us will be judged on how we have lived.

Today’s Gospel is a timely reminder to pause and look honestly at our lives.

Jesus identifies himself with the poor, the lost, the hungry, the naked, the sick and those in prison: all who need our love.

The measure of the final judgement is the love we have shown to these little ones, especially those most in need.

So, we must ask ourselves: how will I fare when that moment comes?

Will I be found wanting in my actions, my kindness, my compassion and my love?

Or will I be judged well because I have loved well, becoming the hands, feet, eyes, ears and arms of Christ, reaching out to those most in need of love?

In the end, nothing else will matter: not how much we own, our status in life or our worldly achievements; not the titles we have held or the success of our careers.

What matters is love for those who need to be loved.
