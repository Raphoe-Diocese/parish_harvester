During my time there I visited the Bearra peninsula, some coastal towns and their local church.

In Castletownbere, one of the major fishing ports in the country, at the back of the church there was a large manuscript on display, with the names of all the people, fishermen in particular, who lost their lives off the coast. The document was indeed a historical record, indicating the long association of the area with this vital means of providing food for the local inhabitants and for people further afield, with the obvious danger involved for the fishermen and sailors in the doing.

On the Thursday, I headed east making my way to Clonakilty where I was looking forward to meeting a friend, now living in a Mercy Sisters Retirement Home.

I passed Bantry and Skibbereen, it was toowet to stop in either town; I continued on, and found myself approaching Ballydehob.

I remembered that a classmate of mine in Maynooth, Liam Hickey came from there.

He is still in ministry near Mayfield, Cork city.

As I drove across the bridge leading into the town, I noticed the church overlooking the town, but I’m damned if I could find its location until eventually, I enquired from a loca l resident as to its whereabouts.

The building, seemed like any traditional small church built in the 30s or 40s, but on entering, I was immediately struck by the sanctuary area, which was painted in a deep blue and red, framed by three beautiful windows allowing the light to enter.

The backdrop to the Eucharistic Table was mainly in blue, highlighting hundreds of gold dots and some lettering.

On closer inspection, I discovered that the writ ten words were a line from the Irish Hymn , Ag Chriost an Síol . The line was, ‘I líontaidh Dé go gcastar sinn’. ‘In God’s net(or embrace) may we dwell’ . The gold dots were actually a shole of wee fish, in wave formation, all focused on the golden Tabe rnacle.

What took my breath away, was the Eucharistic Table, Bord an Tiarna . It consisted of a single slab of rock; it must have weighed at least 5 or 6 tonnes, rugged but perfectly smooth on the top, and obviously taken from the area, which is definitely wanting for stone.

I was immediately aware of the tradition associated with the Mass rock, but what really took my attention, was the liturgical concept of having the Eucharistic Table, made from stone, linked with the New Testament image of Christ depicted as the ‘Cornerstone’ ‘An Cloch Cornéil’. ‘You are part of a building that has Christ Jesus himself for its main c ornerstone’. Ephesians 2:21 It struck me that in terms of Christian Faith, the solid rock symbolised faith as a source of power, hope and comfort for anyone who was blessed to be touched by it.

The fact that the stone was so natural, gave a nice sense that this faith was also hewn not only from the Christian tradition, but also nurtured in and through the local environment and community.

We speak of faith as a gift from God, but that gift is made available through the goodness of family, neighbour and the liturgica l life and lifestyle of the local Church.

Vatican us that the Eucharis t is our primary Communal Prayer, through which we are invited to meet at the Eucharistic Table, Bord an Tiarna as a praying community, a ‘Christian community’ ‘Pobal Chriostaí’ , and in so doing, to be in ‘Communion with our Lord and Saviour’. I will remember my visit to Ballydehob in particular. (Béal an Dá Chab means'mouth of the two river fords') On a lighter note, on the Friday as I headed towards Clonakilty, I stopp ed at a lovely beach to indulge in some go for a long walk.

It was a lovely day, sun shining, but quite a strong breeze blowing as well.

As I made my way back along the beach, listening to Itzhak Perlman play Beethoven’s violin Concerto, on the headphones, I got into the mood, stopped walking and started doing a sort of quickstep from a standing position, ano ther form of exercise.

I wouldn’t say it was a dance, but to my mind and body it was satisfying.

A lady emerged from thes hallow sea with her surfboard; she approached me with a concerned look, ’are you OK?’She asked.

I replied that ‘I was wonde rful, but asked why her concern? ‘She replied, ‘I thought you were having difficulty walking against the stiff bree ze.

I smiled. The kindness of strangers! Saturday evening, I met my classmate, Liam Hickey in Cork.

Over the course of evening, he showed me a book written by his brother, Fr Paddy Hickey with the title ‘Famine in West Cork’ The book was a detailed study, much of the material researched in London, consisting of letters and appeals written from fa mine-stricken hinterlands in the West Cork area, and documentation from the British government, detailing the lax response to p eople living in desperation.

One detail mentioned was that so many desperate people went to the Workhouses, not for she lter but so that they would have at least a burial.

Sunday 25th October 2026 i (30th Sunday in Ordinary time) anai PABAA This week’s Gospel follows close behind the Gospel read last Aya JN sunday.

It is the last of three questions put to Jesus by Jewish eavix religious leaders who are trying to trick him into saying something / that might gethim arrested.

This reminds us that the context for K | today's reading is the mounting tension between Jesus and the religious leaders in Jerusalem. tot Ith The Herodians and the Pharisees asked the first question, which was about taxes.

The Sadducees asked the second question, which was about the Resurrection (see Matthew 22:22-33).

The third question, considered in today’s Gospel, is asked by a Pharisee who asks Jesus about the greatest of the commandments.

The question requires Jesus to interpret the Law of Moses.

The Mosaic Law consists of the Ten Commandments and many additional rules, numbering over six hundred.

Adherence to the Mosaic Law, for a devout Jew, is an expression of faithfulness to God's covenant with Israel.

The ranking of the Commandments was regularly debated among the teachers of the Law.

Jesus answers the Pharisees’ question with a two-fold summary.

Jesus says that all of the commandments can be summarized in two commandments: love God and love your neighbour.

Both of these were central elements of the religious tradition Jesus learned from his Jewish community.

Indeed, these continue to be central aspects of contemporary Jewish religious understanding.

Jesus’ response to his questioners proposed an integral connection between these two aspects of the Jewish Law.

Love of God finds its expression in our love for our neighbour.
