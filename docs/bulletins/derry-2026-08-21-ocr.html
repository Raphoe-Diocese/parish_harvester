Thoughts on the Gospel
Matthew 14:13 –21 – The Feeding of the Five Thousand
The miracle of the loaves and fishes begins with compassion . Before Jesus fed the
crowd, He first recognised their suffering and their need. His heart was moved by those
who were tired, hungry, and searching for hope.
In our own busy lives, God gently calls us to notice those around us who may be lonely,
discouraged, forgotten, or struggling. A simple word of encouragement, a listening ear,
or a small act of kindness may become someone else's miracle.
The disciples looked only at what they didn't have . Faced with thousands of hungry
people, they saw only five loaves and two fish. Jesus, however, asked them to offer what
they already possessed.
God rarely asks us for what we do not have. Instead, He asks us to place before Him our
time, our talents, our compassion, and our willingness to serve. However small these
gifts may seem, He is able to multiply them beyond anything we could imagine.
Before distributing the food, Jesus lifted everything to His Father in prayer. This reminds
us that surrender is not a sign of weakness or defeat. Rather, it is an act of trust. We all
carry burdens that feel too heavy or situations that seem impossible, y et when we place
them into God's hands, what appears insufficient can become more than enough.
Finally, the twelve baskets of leftovers remind us of God's overwhelming generosity. His
blessings are never intended to stop with us but to flow through us. When we forgive
generously, encourage freely, and share what we have with others, God's grace
continues to multiply in countless lives.
Prayer
Lord, fill our hearts with compassion. Use the little we have to accomplish great
things for Your Kingdom. Teach us to trust You completely, to surrender our
limitations into Your hands, and to share Your blessings generously with all whom
we meet. Amen.

Feast Days Monday 29th June: St. Peter and St. Paul were important apostles of Jesus Christ, with distinct backgrounds and roles. St. Peter, an ordinary man, one of the original twelve disciples, was chosen by Jesus and considered the ‘rock’ upon which the Church was to be built. St. Peter was martyred in Rome, likely during Nero’s persecution. St. Paul, initially a persecutor of Christians, underwent a dramatic conversion and became ‘Apostle to the Gentiles’, a zealous missionary spreading Christianity and a prolific writer whose scripts and letters are foundational to Christian theology. St. Paul was martyred in Rome, likely around the same time as St. Peter. Their joint feast signifies their remarkable commitment to spreading the Gospel; their grace and courage to bear witness and the wisdom to shape the early Church. Tuesday 30th June: The First Martyrs of the See of Rome When the city of Rome had been devastated by fire in the year 64, the Emperor Nero launched a persecution against the Christians, who were thrown to the wild beasts in the arena or soaked in tar and used as living torches. Their deaths are documented in the writings of the Roman historian Tacitus and in Pope St. Clement’s letter to the Corinthians. Thoughts on the Gospel (Matthew 14:13 -21)
The miracle of the loaves and fishes began with compassio n. Jesus first noticed the
crowd’s pain before providing for their hunger. In our busy lives, God invites us to see
those who are lonely, discouraged, or forgotten. Even small acts of kindness can
become someone’s miracle.
The disciples focused on what they lacked, yet Jesus asked them to offer what they did
have. God rarely asks for what we do not possess. When we offer our time, talents,
and resources — however small — He multiplies them in ways we cannot imagine.
Before the food was shared, Jesus surrendered everything to the Father. We too carry
burdens that feel heavy, but surrender is not giving up; it is trusting God with our
limitations. What seems insufficient to us becomes more than enough in His hands.
The abundance left over reminds us that God’s blessings are meant to flow through
us. When we share generously, encourage others, and forgive freely, His grace
continues to multiply.
Lord, fill us with compassion, use what little we offer, and multiply it for Your glory.
Teach us to trust You and to share Your blessings generously. Amen.

The Assumption of the Blessed Virgin Mary
15 August 2026
The Taj Mahal has been described as a ‘love song in marble.’
Completed in 1645, the magnificent marble mausoleum was
built by Shah Jahan, India’s Mogul emperor, in memory of his
favorite wife, Mumtaz Mahal (= ‘the chosen one of the
palace’).
Her maiden name was Princess Arjumand. Shah Jahan loved
her deeply, calling her his ‘Taj Mahal,’ meaning ‘The Pearl of
the Palace.’ But Princess Mumtaz Mahal died giving birth to
their fourteenth child, and the emperor was inconsolable.
So, he summoned a great architect from Persia to build the
Taj Mahal, telling him that it must be ‘the one perfect
memorial in the world.’ Seventeen years were needed to build
this enchanting edifice of gleaming white marble embroidered
with flashing jewels. It is an enduring monument to love that
still inspires tourists, artists, and writers from all over the
world.
This beautiful love story gives us some idea of how much God
must have loved Mary, the mother of Jesus. Today’s feast of
her Assumption into Heaven is proof of this.
By raising her from the dead and taking her into Heaven –
body and soul – God demonstrated His undying love for Mary.
Like Shah Jahan, God could not bear the death of His beloved.
However, God could do what no Indian emperor could do –
raise His beloved from the dead and restore her to life even
more beautiful than before.
Moreover, God didn’t have to build a Taj Mahal to memorialize
Mary. Her glorified body is itself a magnificent temple of the
Holy Spirit. (Albert Cylwicki in His Word Resounds).

her.
Maybe this is a ‘change moment’ for Jesus when
he realises through this poor woman that he is
sent not only to his own lost ones, but that his
mission is for the whole world.
This is not the first biblical figure to argue with
the Lord: Genesis and Exodus tell of times when
Abraham and Moses gave out to God. This
woman is different from the scribes who often
tried to trap Jesus with arguments.
She is begging for her daughter. There is
something teasing and humorous about Jesus’
first reply to her. He enjoyed that Semitic style of
thrust and counter.
His first response is what we often experience in
prayer: no give. But a mother seeking health for
her child is not easily deterred, and Jesus rewards
her persistence – I imagine him smiling as he

Thoughts on the Gospel
Matthew 14:13 –21 – The Feeding of the Five Thousand
The miracle of the loaves and fishes begins with compassion . Before Jesus fed the
crowd, He first recognised their suffering and their need. His heart was moved by those
who were tired, hungry, and searching for hope.
In our own busy lives, God gently calls us to notice those around us who may be lonely,
discouraged, forgotten, or struggling. A simple word of encouragement, a listening ear,
or a small act of kindness may become someone else's miracle.
The disciples looked only at what they didn't have . Faced with thousands of hungry
people, they saw only five loaves and two fish. Jesus, however, asked them to offer what
they already possessed.
God rarely asks us for what we do not have. Instead, He asks us to place before Him our
time, our talents, our compassion, and our willingness to serve. However small these
gifts may seem, He is able to multiply them beyond anything we could imagine.
Before distributing the food, Jesus lifted everything to His Father in prayer. This reminds
us that surrender is not a sign of weakness or defeat. Rather, it is an act of trust. We all
carry burdens that feel too heavy or situations that seem impossible, y et when we place
them into God's hands, what appears insufficient can become more than enough.
Finally, the twelve baskets of leftovers remind us of God's overwhelming generosity. His
blessings are never intended to stop with us but to flow through us. When we forgive
generously, encourage freely, and share what we have with others, God's grace
continues to multiply in countless lives.
Prayer
Lord, fill our hearts with compassion. Use the little we have to accomplish great
things for Your Kingdom. Teach us to trust You completely, to surrender our
limitations into Your hands, and to share Your blessings generously with all whom
we meet. Amen.

REST A WHILE
In our day -to-day life’s journey, oftentimes we are overwhelmed with
our daily routines, individual tasks as well as duties and responsibilities.
It seems that the 24 hours of the day are not enough because we are too
busy with so many things. We do not have time to “stop and smell the
roses.” We do not have time to relax and to take time out of our busy
schedules to appreciate and enjoy God ’s gift of the beauty of life. We
need time to rest, to recognises our human limitations that we need essential respite to
prevent us from being exhausted and to avoid the possible danger of breakdown.
According to the mechanical engineers, even machines need cooling -off time to prevent
overheating. When we approach our “hallowed space ” like spending time praying in front
of the Blessed Sacrament or lighting candles inside the church, we are refreshed and
strengthened. We will be inspired to go on despite all the burdens, challenges, and
obstacles in life. That is why after the tiresome and successful mission of the Apostles the
Lord told them, “Come away by yourselves to a deserted place and rest a while. ”
Following Jesus ’ command, these first followers were spiritually and physically recharged
to do the next job. We also badly need to have some “refreshment ” of the Lord and to
spend time with Him in silent prayer to find meaning in every life ’s situation. In this way,
we are far from being “worn out” and preventing ourselves from “falling apart. ” And so,
let us all find time to relax and rest for a while with Him.

EVEN A CRUMB OF MERC Y IS ENOUGH
Matthew 15:21–28 tells of the Canaanite woman who begs Jesus to heal her
daughter. At first, Jesus seems silent and distant. Even the disciples want her
sent away. Yet she refuses to leave. Her prayer is simple and urgent: “Lord,
help me.”
When Jesus speaks about the children ’s bread not being thrown to the house -
dogs, she answers with remarkable humility and trust: “Even house -dogs can
eat the scraps that fall from their master ’s table.” She does not insist that she
deserves anything. She simply believes that even the smallest crumb of God ’s
mercy is enough to transform her daughter ’s life.
Jesus responds: “Woman, you have great faith. Let your wish be granted. ”
This Gospel teaches us that God ’s silence is not necessarily God ’s absence.
Sometimes prayer appears unanswered, but faith continues to call upon him.
The woman moves through silence, apparent rejection and humiliation
without surrendering her trust.
She also crosses every boundary: she is a woman, a foreigner and an outsider.
Yet Jesus reveals that faith —not race, status or belonging —opens the heart to
God’s saving mercy.
A prayer for the week
“Lord, help me. Even a crumb of your mercy is enough .”

The Famine Bell
We have recently acquired, through the good
auspices of the Late Father Liam Donnelly, a
very old church bell. This bell was cast during
the famine years.
It is hoped to place the bell in the old
cemetery near the old church ruin in memory
of the victims of the Great Famine, many of
whom are buried here. It is also a memorial to
all victims of conflict in the years since the
building of the Church in 1803.
The bell also reminds us that the law of the land forbade Church
bells when the old church was built. This was one of the Penal
Laws.
Going further back, it also links us to the Great St. Patrick’s Bell,
which was for many years concealed in Mayogall for safety.

Thoughts on the Gospel (Matthew 14:13 -21)
The miracle of the loaves and fishes began with compassio n. Jesus first noticed the
crowd’s pain before providing for their hunger. In our busy lives, God invites us to see
those who are lonely, discouraged, or forgotten. Even small acts of kindness can
become someone’s miracle.
The disciples focused on what they lacked, yet Jesus asked them to offer what they did
have. God rarely asks for what we do not possess. When we offer our time, talents,
and resources — however small — He multiplies them in ways we cannot imagine.
Before the food was shared, Jesus surrendered everything to the Father. We too carry
burdens that feel heavy, but surrender is not giving up; it is trusting God with our
limitations. What seems insufficient to us becomes more than enough in His hands.
The abundance left over reminds us that God’s blessings are meant to flow through
us. When we share generously, encourage others, and forgive freely, His grace
continues to multiply.
Lord, fill us with compassion, use what little we offer, and multiply it for Your glory.
Teach us to trust You and to share Your blessings generously. Amen.

Today’s Gospel contains an exchange between Jesus and a Canaanite woman in which Jesus

self-love, racism and the false notion of selected supremacy! What happens is that a child

Canaanite woman. He sees her love for her tormented child. He sees her willingness to
suffer insult for the sake of her child. And he sees her unflinching faith in him as her child’s
only hope.

Thoughts on the Gospel
Matthew 14:13 –21 – The Feeding of the Five Thousand
The miracle of the loaves and fishes begins with compassion . Before Jesus fed the
crowd, He first recognised their suffering and their need. His heart was moved by those
who were tired, hungry, and searching for hope.
In our own busy lives, God gently calls us to notice those around us who may be lonely,
discouraged, forgotten, or struggling. A simple word of encouragement, a listening ear,
or a small act of kindness may become someone else's miracle.
The disciples looked only at what they didn't have . Faced with thousands of hungry
people, they saw only five loaves and two fish. Jesus, however, asked them to offer what
they already possessed.
God rarely asks us for what we do not have. Instead, He asks us to place before Him our
time, our talents, our compassion, and our willingness to serve. However small these
gifts may seem, He is able to multiply them beyond anything we could imagine.
Before distributing the food, Jesus lifted everything to His Father in prayer. This reminds
us that surrender is not a sign of weakness or defeat. Rather, it is an act of trust. We all
carry burdens that feel too heavy or situations that seem impossible, y et when we place
them into God's hands, what appears insufficient can become more than enough.
Finally, the twelve baskets of leftovers remind us of God's overwhelming generosity. His
blessings are never intended to stop with us but to flow through us. When we forgive
generously, encourage freely, and share what we have with others, God's grace
continues to multiply in countless lives.
Prayer
Lord, fill our hearts with compassion. Use the little we have to accomplish great
things for Your Kingdom. Teach us to trust You completely, to surrender our
limitations into Your hands, and to share Your blessings generously with all whom
we meet. Amen.

“Woman, you have great faith”
The Canaanite woman in the Gospel appears almost to be the opposite
of Peter from last week whom Jesus accused of having little faith. As an
outsider, she comes to him with a mother’s urgent prayer: “Lord, help
me.” At first her plea seems to meet silence and resistance. Yet she does
not walk away: she remains humble, courageous, and persistent,
trusting that even the smallest share in God’s mercy is enough to bring
healing.
Her faith it is not loud in a demanding entitled way. She displays a strong
faith in the sense of refusing to give up on Jesus. She, an outsider,
recognises him as Lord and places before him the suffering of someone
she loves. Many of us will know something of that prayer: the prayer
made beside a hospital bed, for a family member in difficulty, for a child
who has lost their way, or for a burden that we carry that seems slow to
lift.
Jesus’ final words to her are full of hope: “Woman, you have great faith.”
The Gospel reminds us that God’s mercy is wider than our boundaries.
The first reading speaks of God’s house as “a house of prayer for all
peoples,” and the Gospel shows that promise beginning to unfold in the
life of Christ. No one who turns to the Lord with faith is beyond his
compassion.
This Gospel is both comforting and challenging. It comforts us by
teaching us to persevere in prayer, even when answers seem delayed. It
challenges us to become a community where those who feel distant,
overlooked, or outside the usual circle can still encounter welcome,
dignity, and mercy. This week, let us pray with the Canaanite woman’s
simple words: “Lord, help me.” And let us ask for the grace to see others
with the generous heart of Christ.
