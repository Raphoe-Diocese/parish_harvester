PRAYER TO ST. MONICA
Lord Jesus,
you were moved by the prayers and tears of St. Monica
and you gave the grace of conversion to St. Augustine your son.
Touch our hearts and convert us from fear to love,
from sickness to health and from conflict to peace of mind.
Bless all mothers, living or dead, known or unknown,
married, single or widowed.
May our lives draw others to love you.
Lord, it was at your altar that Saint Monica wanted to be remembered.
(St. Monica - Feast Day: 27th August )
PRAYER TO ST. AUGUST INE
Holy Father Augustine,
saint and once a sinner, pray for us sinners.
Help us to imitate you in seeking God with all our hearts,
in seeking the grace of repentance and renewal of life.
Help us to Love God and neighbour at all times.
Help us to realise, as you did,
that we are made for God
and that our hearts will be restless,
always restless, until they rest in Him. (St. Augustine - Feast Day: 28th
August)
PRAYER TO ST. JOHN T HE BAPTIST
O God, You raised up St. John the Baptist
to prepare a perfect people for Christ.
Fill Your people with the joy
of possessing His grace,
and direct the minds of all the faithful
in the way of peace and salvation.
Grant that as St. John was martyred
for truth and justice,
so we may energetically profess our Faith in You,
and lead others to the Way, the Truth, and Eternal Life. Amen.
(The Passion of St. John the Baptist - 29th August)

Reflection
In today ’s Gospel we experience Peter firmly identifying Jesus as the “Christ, the Son of the living
God. ” God does not always choose the holiest to govern and to serve in His Church. Our Lord
chose Peter as Chief of the Apostles even though he was subject to many imperfections. Peter,
filled with much zeal, was apt to be impetuous. While he followed our Savior with his whole heart,
he stumbled many times after his initial calling. He boasted that he would never abandon Our Lord.
Yet, he found himself cursing Him and saying that he never knew Him. That pierced our Lord ’s
heart!
Yet, Our Lord did not reject Peter, since He was sure that St. Peter had a strong and constant
determination to correct himself. Peter ’s natural disposition to cater to his feelings and desires
was in part the cause of his lapses. When we experience certain lapses in our on -going conversion, we must not abandon
our quest for holiness. Like Peter, let us have a strong and constant determination to take the measures needed to correct
ourselves. Then we too will receive special favours and blessings on earth and in heaven.
We must not listen to the voice that tells us that our faults are unpardonable. We must say boldly that our God died for all.
No matter how ungodly one is, he or she will find redemption in our Saviour. Let us consider with what patience our divine
Saviour awaits those who reject Him. Then like Peter, we may say, “You are the Christ, the Son of the living God, ”
our Redeemer.

Thoughts on the Readings for the 19th Sunday August 16th 2026
Today in the Gospel, we have a continuation of last week’s story about the feeding
of thousands of people by Jesus in the desert. Immediately after the event, we are
told that Jesus: …made the disciples get into a boat and go on ahead to the other
side, while he dismissed the crowds. Was there reluctance on their part to go? Cer-
tainly there is the implication that the disciples were not too willing to leave the scene. They were en-
joying the reflected glory of being part of Jesus’ ‘miracle’ and the enthusiasm of the crowds for Jesus,
‘their’ Jesus. They were basking in the reputation of being partners with Jesus. Yet, it won’t be very
long before they will be hiding, even denying under oath, ever having had connection with him. Jesus
himself, after having dismissed the crowds: …went up the mountain by himself to pray. In John’s ver-
sion of this story he tells us that the people, after being fed by Jesus, actually wanted to make him their
king. They, like the disciples, have totally missed the meaning of what has happened. Here indeed was
a real source of temptation. Jesus could easily have convinced himself that here was a golden opportu-
nity to get control of the crowds and ‘save’ them. They were so ready to follow him —it seemed. The
world was at his feet. There is an echo here of one of the temptations in the desert after his baptism: …
the devil took him to a very high mountain and showed him all the kingdoms of the world and their
glory, and he said to him, “All these I will give you, if you will fall down and worship me.”

Step by Step through the Mass
The Liturgy of the Eucharist (Part 7)
The next small ritual that the priest carries out is known as the Lavabo – which derives from the
Latin word for washing. In this ritual the priest has water poured over his hands by a server or
he dips his hands into a bowl of water. This is not a hygiene ritual – though perhaps in the
distant past it may he begun life as a necessary hand washing. In the distant past (as in some
Missionary Lands to this day) not only the Bread and Wine (and offertory collection) are
brought forward at this stage of the Mass, but also many other gifts of food, oil, meat or dairy
produce are brought forward – often to be distributed later to the poor. This could be grubby
business and would have necessitated that the priest, who received these gifts, had to wash his
hands.
However, this is not the primary reason or purpose for this ritual. It takes its inspiration from
the ritual washing that the Jewish Temple Priests carried out before they offered the sacrifices
to God in the Temple. That ritual washing in the Old Testament Law of Moses was mandatory
for priests every time they would offer a sacrifice. Its meaning was clearly a physical act
seeking to communicate a spiritual reality. It was essential for maintaining the sanctity
required to approach God and perform sacred duties. The hands of a person are symbolic of
their actions – and so the washing of the hands by the priest in a sense reminded him (and those
who witnessed it) that not all his works are good, holy and sinless, since he is a mortal man and
therefore prone to sin. However, he washes his hands now to show that they are being prepared
to do something good and holy – the offering of sacrifice to God. In the Old Testament, when
the people saw a priest ritually wash his hands – they understood that a sacrifice was about to
take place. For us at the Holy Sacrifice of the Mass, when we see the priest perform this simple
ritual which could go unnoticed because it is so brief, we know that the reason for our gathering
at the Church at this Mass is now, in a sense, moving up a gear – the Sacrifice of Jesus on
Calvary is about to be made present in the here and now of our celebration of this Mass.
The priest accompanies this ritual action with a short prayer which he speaks almost inaudibly:
“Wash me, O Lord, from my iniquity and cleanse me from my sin.” It reinforces the fact that
this action is not about physical hygiene, but about being pure in heart and holy in soul –
treating the Holy Things of God in a holy way and with holy fear of the Lord. It is in some way
akin to a little last examination of conscience for the priest before he advances any further in the
offering of Holy Mass.
