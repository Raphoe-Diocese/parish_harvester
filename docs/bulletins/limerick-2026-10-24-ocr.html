Addressing a recent Diocese of Limerick Annual Assembly, Bishop Leahy said that the importance of working together can be seen in many areas of life and pointed to the success of the Limerick senior hurling team as a powerful local example of what can be achieved through teamwork, commitment and a shared spirit.

Working together, he said, is what builds strong, vibrant communities that shelter, encourage and nurture. “You can imagine the Limerick hurling team wouldn’t getfar if they just saw themselves as 15 individual players, ” Bishop Leahy said. ”What makes the team great is the teamwork and spirit.

We know they work on that.” Reflecting on the theme of togetherness, the Bishop said that faith is not simply a private matter but one that calls people into community and service of others.

He encouraged parish communities to continue strengthening local connections, building welcoming environments and supporting one another in faith and daily life.“It’s when we act as community we are at our best. Think of how we celebrate together as a people, the joy we get in that.

Think also of we come together to support people at times of need and the support we give in that,” he said. “God didn’t save us simply as individuals.

He saved us as a people, to be the ‘People of God’,” he said. ”For Christians, there is a triangle we need to remember: me, my neighbour and God. ” The diocesan gathering brought together clergy, parish volunteers, ministry leaders and people involved in a wide range of parish and diocesan initiatives.

The programme highlighted the breadth of activity taking place across the Diocese, with participants encouraged by the scale of engagement and outreach underway in local communities.

Sun 25th Oct 10 am 11.30am Pat Molan RIP (3rd A) Oonagh Bermingham RIP (9th A) Mass Intentions Reflection The Pharisees and Herodians set out to trap Jesus.

They hate him and they want to discredit him and gethim into trouble.

They start out unctuous, smarmy way by building Jesus up attempting to appeal to human pride: You are an honest man; you are not afraid of anyone; aman’s rank means nothing to you.

Then comes their treacherous question: Is it permissible to paytax es to Caesar or not?Yes or no, what is your opinion?

Before we come to his answer we should look at the background.

Jesus and his questioners were living in a country occupied by a hated foreign power; the Romans.

They were hated because of the degradation they caused - no one likes being a slave or tenant in one’s own country - and in addition to that the Romans were plundering the country principally be extorting exor bitant taxes.

We all know well that the tax-collector of the time was especially despised.

We should also consider to whom these taxes were being paid. - Tiberius Caesar, who, in his personal life, was utterly depraved and immoral and who reigned by terror, who was a murderer and who, as emperor, claimed to be a god and oppressed and enslaved millions of people.

Now to Jesus’ answer. If he had said: don’tpay taxes to that evil wretch, he would have fallen foul of the Roman authorities.

If he said pay the taxes, he would have been characterized as pro-Roman and would have fallen foul of his own oppressed people.

His questioners thought they had him in a no-win bind.

Then of course he gave the masterful answer. “Let me see the money you pay the taxes with.

Whose head is this whose name. Very well, give back to Caesar what belongs to Caesar. ” Then followed the far more important part: “and to God what belongs to God. ” Here Jesus is declaring that obedience to God and reverence f or God comes before any other consideration including civil, secular society.

The Catechism has this to say about it:“The citizen is obliged in conscience not to follow the directives of civil authorities when they are contrary to the demands of the moral order, to the fundamental rights of persons or the teach- ings of the Gospel.

Refusing obedience to civil authorities , when their demands are contrary to those of an upright conscience, finds its justificati on in the distinction between serving God and serving the political communi ty. “Render therefore to Caesar the things that are Caesar and to God the things that are God’s.” “We must obey God rather than men. ” (Acts 5:29) (CCC 2242) The Second Vatican Council had this to say: “When citizens are under the op- pression of a public authority which oversteps its competence, they should still not refuse to give or to do what is objectively demanded of them by t he common good; but it is legitimate for them to defend their own rights and those of their fellow citizens against the abuse of this authority within the limits of the natural law and the Law of the Gospel. ” (GS 74. 5) We are obliged to be good citizens but when secular society commands, permits and promoted what is gravely morally wrong we have not only the right but the duty to raise o ur voices in objection because our first loyalty belongs to God.

World Mission Sunday is the Holy Father’s annual appeal for spiritual and financial support so that the life-giving work of overseas mission and missionaries can continue.

It always falls on the second last Sunday in October, and like the Good Friday collection for the Holy Land and Peter’s Pence, it is one of three yearly universal Church collections.

World Mission Sunday collections take place in every single parish where the Church is present.
