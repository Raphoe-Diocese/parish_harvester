Reflection
Today ’s gospel recounts an unusual exchange between Jesus and the unnamed Canaanite woman
who challenges him. She has seen what Jesus can do, she has faith, and she is advocating for her
daughter. She is persistent, shouting out to Jesus and apparently also badgering the disciples, to the
point where they plead with Jesus to ‘give her what she wants. ’ Jesus ’ rebuke seems shockingly
unkind: ‘It is not fair to take the children ’s food and throw it to the house -dogs ’.
But the woman doesn ’t give up. It is a matter of justice for her, as is evident in her witty response
about the dogs getting the scraps from the master ’s table.
It’s a strange story, with Jesus at first appearing cold in his response to the woman. But as we know,
he has a tendency to test his followers. His disciples, who know him well and have seen lots of miracles, often fall short. B ut
this woman passes with flying colours. Jesus can see that she is a person of strong faith, clever and determined. She is not
easily dismissed. In allowing her to state her case, Jesus also presents a challenge to the religious and social status quo.
This pagan woman (Jesus ’ reference to ‘house -dogs ’ tells us a lot about her status) exhibits more faith than his own
Jesus does not want us to be silent, compliant or complacent. He rejoices in those who are brave and persistent,
and eager to challenge the injustices they see around them – those who have the gift of changing minds and hearts.
We might call it the gift of prophecy. Tríona Doherty

remove the three -day or 72 -hour waiting period between a mother seeing
her GP and proceeding with an abortion. This waiting period creates a small
but vital space for the mother to reconsider her decision and thereby save
the life of her unborn child. We know that the waiting period has saved lives,
because between 2019 and 2024 more than 10,000 expectant mothers did
not proceed with an abortion. That number, having contacted their GP and
begun the abortion process, did not return after the three -day period had
elapsed. Two of the 86 TDs who voted to remove the three -day waiting
period are from our constituency. We voted for them. You and I must now
persuade them to change their minds before the Bill returns to the Dail in the
Autumn for the final vote. It is the least that mothers and their unborn
children deserve.

Thoughts on the Readings for the 19th Sunday August 16th 2026
Today in the Gospel, we have a continuation of last week’s story about the feeding
of thousands of people by Jesus in the desert. Immediately after the event, we are
told that Jesus: …made the disciples get into a boat and go on ahead to the other
side, while he dismissed the crowds. Was there reluctance on their part to go? Cer-
tainly there is the implication that the disciples were not too willing to leave the scene. They were en-
joying the reflected glory of being part of Jesus’ ‘miracle’ and the enthusiasm of the crowds for Jesus,
‘their’ Jesus. They were basking in the reputation of being partners with Jesus. Yet, it won’t be very
long before they will be hiding, even denying under oath, ever having had connection with him. Jesus
himself, after having dismissed the crowds: …went up the mountain by himself to pray. In John’s ver-
sion of this story he tells us that the people, after being fed by Jesus, actually wanted to make him their
king. They, like the disciples, have totally missed the meaning of what has happened. Here indeed was
a real source of temptation. Jesus could easily have convinced himself that here was a golden opportu-
nity to get control of the crowds and ‘save’ them. They were so ready to follow him —it seemed. The
world was at his feet. There is an echo here of one of the temptations in the desert after his baptism: …
the devil took him to a very high mountain and showed him all the kingdoms of the world and their
glory, and he said to him, “All these I will give you, if you will fall down and worship me.”

Step by Step through the Mass
The Liturgy of the Eucharist (Part 6)
We have seen how the mingling of the water into the wine in the chalice evokes for us the
union we should have, and should aspire to ever increasingly have, with Jesus. It can be an
important moment for us to once again examine our conscience: “The Water and the Wine are
now in an inseparable union in that chalice. Are my heart and mind, is my soul, in an equally
inseparable union with Christ Jesus as I participate in this Mass? Additionally, if the Wine in
the Chalice represents Christ’s offering of him self; and the water represents my human
offering, what am I bringing to this Mass today? Obviously, Christ’s prayer is greater than
mine; His praise of the Heavenly Father more perfect than mine; his love for God and
Neighbour (shown by the outpouring of his Blood on the Cross) greater than my love. But
what I do offer, meagre as it is, I offer through Jesus, with Jesus and in Jesus.
Perhaps I carry in my heart to this Mass some person or situation in my life that needs God’s
grace or intervention. As I see the water added to the wine, I can remember that I place what
is in and on my heart into and upon the Heart of Jesus now – my pra yer mingles with his, my
intentions he takes upon himself to present it all before the Face of the Heavenly Father. All
of this, and more, can be set before our minds at this brief moment when the Water is mixed
into the Wine.
Once the chalice has been offered (with the accompanying offertory prayer), the priest then
bows low and quietly prays the following prayer on his behalf and on behalf of those
participating at the Mass: “ With humble spirit and contrite heart may we be accepted by you,
O Lord, and may our sacrifice in your sight this day be pleasing to you, Lord God. ” This brief
prayer is an acknowledgment of his and our unworthiness and need for repentance as we
spiritually and really approach the Heavenly Father in this Mass. The words “sacrifice in your
sight” remind us that the Holy Sacrifice of the Mass gives us privileged and unparalleled access
to God the Father. Which is why our ancestors, (who knew these truths, though perhaps they
would not have been able to articulate them in theological terms), would move heaven and
earth to be physically present at Mass – even if for generations during persecution their
presence meant risking life and limb just to be at Mass. How sad it is then that so many,
physically fit and healthy people, now cannot motivate themselves to get out of bed on any
given Sunday to make their appointment with the Living God at Mass. Fr Philip Kemmy, PP Fr. Damien Nejad, CC
