Newsletter for the weekend of 3-4 October 2026. Reflection on the Gospel for the 27th Sunday in Ordinary Time. This Sunday’s Gospel invites us into the story of a landowner who lovingly prepares a vineyard and entrusts it to tenants. Itisa picture of generosity: everything is given, everything is ready, and the tenants are simply asked to work with what they have received. Yet when the landowner sends his servants to collect the harvest, the tenants re- spond with resentment and violence. They reject every messenger, and finally even thesonhim- self. It is easy to dismiss them as villains, but Jesus tells this parable to hold up a mirror. The vineyard isourown lives: our families, our parish, our gifts, our opportunities, even our world. God entrusts these things tousnotas owners, butasten- ants —caretakers who are meant to collaborate with His grace. When we forget that life is a gift, we begin to cling toitasa possession. We defend our time, our comfort, our plans, and we resist anything that challenges them. Yet God continues to send “messengers” into our lives. They may come as people who need our pa- tience, situations that stretch us, or truths we would rather avoid. Sometimes the messenger is simply reality itself —a setback, a disappoint- ment, a moment of honesty. Wecanbe tempted to reject these messengers, but they are often the very means through which God shapes us. The Gospel ends with a promise: the stone re- jected by the builders becomes the cornerstone. God builds His kingdom not on what we proudly present, butonthe very places we resist or fear. The question for us this week is simple: Are we living as owners… or as tenants? Tenants receive, collaborate, and give back. May we welcome God’s messengers and bear fruit worthy of the vineyard entrusted to us. Many accept, without reflection, that our prisons are | <j full of dangerous, violent criminals. While there are certainly some dangerous people in prison, 80% of those convicted are sentenced to 12 months or less, suggesting that their crimes are less serious (accepting that even less serious crimes can be upsetting or even traumatic for the victim). 70% of prisoners have an addiction but our addiction services have long waiting lists. The Government plan to provide 1,500 extra prison places to combat the overcrowding in our prisons, but it might actually be more effective to provide 1,500 extra drug treatment places. 70% have mental health issues and our mental health services are generally recognised as very inadequate. Our prisons are overcrowded, full of drugs and increasingly violent places, making rehabilitation almost impossible. Some are on remand with bail set at €50 or €100, but they haven't gotit, so they remain in prison simply because they are poor. Others have bail, but because they cannot provide an address, they remain in prison because they are homeless. Most people who commit crime grow out of it. This weekly ceremony was for the women of the parish at 7.30pm on Monday evenings and for themenon Tuesday. Parishioners avoided appearing in public during Confraternity times if they were not attending. The ceremony consisted of rosary and a short sermon followed by Benediction. The congregation was divided into sections, more or less by street, and each section had a banner held in position by the rings. Our section was St. Aidan ’s, Michael Street, and our leader was Pete Brothers, whose jobitwasto mark the attendance book which no doubt was examined by the clergy now and again to note non-attenders. The final hymn was always “Happy we who thus united. ” which was sung with gusto. The second half of the first line was “. joined in cheerful melody ”. Pete used to delight in singing “ .joined in fearful melody ”. Courtesy of Jim Sutton
