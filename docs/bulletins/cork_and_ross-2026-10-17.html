Beyond telling us a liƩle about his own vocation story and narrating the appointments he’s held since being ordained, Marius also reached out for people to form a monthly prayer meeting specifically for vocations to the priesthood in the diocese of Cork and Ross.
Some 56 people signed up in Ballincollig, which I think is quite encouraging.
The Church does need some of its members to serve communities as ordained priests and praying for this is worthwhile, not least because it reminds us to be open to God’s will in our lives.
That said, though, I do wonder about the bigger picture.
If a lack of vocations were down to a lack of prayer, then the issue would never have arisen: each and every day people tell me that they pray for their priests and for vocations.
So, a reasonable person would naturally be led to ask if the Holy Spirit is prompting us to change how we are as a Church.
And while no one person can speak definitively for the Spirit, the ‘sensus fidelium’ [‘the shared sense of the faithful’] is worth paying heed to.
I know myself that hardly a week goes by without someone asking me about the potential role of married men and women.
These are legitimate questions to ask, even if a convincing answer comes frustratingly slowly.
In the meantime, it might be good to ask ourselves what role I can exercise in my local parish: what gifts do I have to share?
There is ever and always a need for additional voices to read, more hands to minister Holy Communion, new minds to share vision at the Parish Pastoral Council.
All that’s asked is that be open to God’s will in our lives – both in what’s necessary now, and what’s possible in the future.

If only it were as straightforward for people!
Unlike aeroplanes, we tend to be victims of clouds, be they meteorological or psychological in nature.
When you are looking down from an aeroplane window, all you can see for seemingly endless miles are the brilliantly white, marshmallow like clouds.
If you look through the breaks in the cloud, you might see the darker underbelly of the clouds, the rain falling from them and the shadows they cast. You can imagine the people underneath them being pretty miserable.
I’m sure we’ve all experienced a dark, cloudy day.
But it doesn't have to be that way. Consider it this way instead . The sun is actually still shining brightly through the majority of the sky.
There’s just some low-level, insubstantial stuff getting in the way.
On the dull days, take the higher view. God’s love is still most of the sky.
We just need, somehow, to blow away those clouds.
There is always hope. It is real and loving and, like a guardian angel, it walks beside us every day.
If we don’t see it or feel it often enough, it’s only because we’ve let too many other things get in the way.

The Illusion of the Ego When we look at human existence through the eyes of the world, we are taught to measure worth by what we accumulate, what we conquer, and how high we can elevate ourselves.
But the gospel calls us to a different standard.
In the economy of salvation, the ultimate triumph of a soul is not the building up of the self, but the quiet, courageous surrender of the ego to God.
Sacred Scripture reminds us in the Gospel of Matthew, "Whoever exalts himself will be humbled, and whoever humbles himself will be exalted.
The human ego is a restless entity. It constantly demands to be right, to be noticed, to be validated, and to be first. Saint John the Baptist captured the essence of the managed ego when he said of Jesus, "He must increase, but I must decrease.
When we clear away the heavy clutter of pride, we create a void —not an empty emptiness, but a space that can finally be filled by Christ’s light.
The souls who truly impact us in this life are rarely the loudest or the most self-absorbed.
They are the ones who make room for others because they have first made room for God.
They remind us that true dignity doesn't come from a puffed-up sense of importance, but from a surrendered heart . It takes immense faith to trust that God sees our quiet goodness, even when the world overlooks it.

Persistence in prayer demonstrates that we are not afraid to show our dependence on God even though all around us society says we need be self-sufficient, to look after ourselves.
To keep praying is equally looking after ourselves but also the needs of those around us and the needs of our world.
The widow in the story is concerned about the delay in getting a response, however we must not only must persevere in prayer we must be patient, awaiting God’s time for an answer and accepting the answer even though it may not be one we wanted!
God will always give the right answer to our prayers, even if in the immediate moment it may not seem that way.
Take time to reflect on what have been the fruits of your own persistent prayer?

Reflection on Today’s Gospel: What does the term ‘apostle’ mean?
It is someone who is sent. Jesus himself was sent by the Father to reveal God to the world and to redeem humanity.
Jesus chose twelve of his followers and they acted as his core group of collaborators.
Their closeness to Jesus over the time of his public ministry was a preparation for their mission in life.
Just as Jesus had been sent by the Father, Jesus in turn sent out his twelve disciples to make him known, initially to the Jews and eventually to the whole world.
In today’s gospel he tells them: ‘And as you go, proclaim that the kingdom of heaven is close at hand’ (Matthew 10:7). Later in that gospel we read t he Great Commission, Jesus’ final sending out of his apost les: All authority in heaven and on earth has been given to me.
Go therefore and make disciples of all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit, and teaching them to obey everything that I have commanded you.
And remember, I am with you always, to the end of the age(Mt 28:18-20) This commission is now the responsibility of the whole Church , not just the successors of the apostles.
Pope Francis proposed this extension of the apostolic mission to all Christians as synodality.
Pope Leo has continued this emphasis on synodality from the beginning of his papacy.
