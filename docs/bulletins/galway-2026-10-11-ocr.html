Equinox refers to mid-autumn, around the 21st September, when day and night are equal, a brief period of rest and pause, inviting us to stop and draw a breath if we choose todoso. Strictly speaking, 'equilux' comes first, the time of 'equal light,' when dayl ight and night-time are almost completely equal. Equinox means equal darkness, which occurs adayorso later, and refers to the moment when the sun crosses the earth's equator. In September, the sun enters Libra, the sign of balance, reminding us especially to consider whether our own lives are in balance andhowwe wish to move into the next phase of the year. Dates of the equinox can vary slightly year by year. This slightly bittersweet f estival of farewell to summer and facing into winter is observed by many different cultures around the world in different ways. Here in Ireland, it was traditionally a break for the busy farmers harvesting crops before the coming winter. Leg ends of the 'Púca,' ruining the blackberries before Michaelmas remind us to gather our harvest nowandnot wait until nature deteriorates in the coming cold. The Greeks remember the story of Persephone's annual descent to Hades, leaving her mother Demeter l onely until spring. Native Americans prepare crops for winter, the Chinese observe a mid-autumn festival and Jews celebrate the weekend holiday of Sukkot, a harvest thanksgiving. Some refer to this festival as Mabon and consider it a powerful time for prayer and meditation. Equinox is also known as Michaelmas, the feast of Archangel Michael, 'hewhois like God’ , in which courage triumphs over fear and the ferocious dragon who threatens the town is defeated, not slain bybrute force, but brought by the power of inner strength. Traditionally and still in some areas, such as Waldorf sc hools, children perform pageants about St. George's taming of the dragon by drawing on his inner power and strength under the influence o f Archangel Michael. This story isnottosay that we need toseethe same dragon as evil; rather, it symbolises a diffi cult part of ourselves or our lives that we can overcome if we have faith in God and draw upon our strength and courage. Children are t aught to intone the rhyme 'Brave and true/ Will I be/ Each good deed/ Sets me free/ I will fight/ for the right/ I will conquer the wrong.' We could choose to celebrate Michaelmas primarily by accepting its in vitation to stop and draw breath. We could use our labyrinth as a walking meditation to turn our attention inwards for a spell . We could also goforan intentional nature walk through the autumn landscape, and give thanks for the beauty of the natural world, as well asforthe blessings inourown lives. We could also consider preparing feasts with our loved ones us ing seasonal produce such as apples, pumpkins, blackberries and grains. As we observed during our recent Lunar eclipse, we could take this time to consider our own 'dragons,' or as Carl Jung would see them, our shadow selves, harmful if left unchecked but potentially a powerful source of goodn essifwecan control it with our own will.
